# In-depth research: quantum equation terms and ancient concepts

## 1. \(i\hbar \partial_t\Psi\) — the pulse as origin
In Indian traditions *spanda* means divine inner "pulsation," not a physical movement in the outer world. Kshemarāja explains that *spanda* is "a Divine Pulsation, but not in a physical sense… a movement in the space of Consciousness." Analogously, the Śiva Sūtras state that consciousness (*caitanya*) is active (sutra I.1: *caitanyam ātman*), i.e., a dynamic state of perception. In other words, primordial reality is not static silence but an inner "pulse" of the divine (Śiva/Consciousness) that self-illuminates.

- **Unstruck vs. struck Nāda:** Tantric texts like the *Nāda-bindu Upaniṣad* and the *Hamsa Upaniṣad* distinguish *nāda anāhata* (unstruck, inner sound) from outer sound. For example, the *Hamsa Upaniṣad* states that when Hamsa absorbs itself in *nāda* (spiritual sound), it reaches a state "beyond sound, beyond speech and mind," equating it with Brahman. That is, before manifestation there is a subtle vibration (a "primordial sound") preceding every sonic object, not absolute void.
- **Pre-cosmic space in Philolaus:** In ancient Greece, Philolaus proposed that creation arises from combining "limiters" and "unlimiteds" ordered only by a harmony (the Pythagorean **harmonia**). Pythagorean fragments say that without this ordering by harmony, those "different and unrelated things" could not give rise to the cosmos. This suggests that "before the world" there was a chaos pending attunement, akin to the Vedic idea of initial *tapas*, not pure silence.

**Answer to the question:** Many traditions emphasize the inner **pulse** (vibration or energy) as origin, not mere silence. The "before the world" is seen more as a state of objectless vibration — a primordial impulse (sometimes called *tapas* or *nāda*) — than a complete void.

## 2. \(-\hbar^2/2m\nabla^2\) — local coupling, continuous medium
This term denotes how each field (or space) point influences its neighbors, typical of waves in a continuous medium. Ancient Vedic texts work extensively with cords (*sulba*) as local measuring instruments: for example, the Śulba Sūtras describe how a stretched cord over a square relates its diagonal to the areas formed. As Baudhayana's Śulba-sūtra observed: *"La cuerda que se extiende a lo largo de la diagonal produce un área que los lados vertical y horizontal hacen juntos."* — i.e., the cord (local operator) "executes" point by point the geometric transformation (Pythagorean theorem).

In ancient acoustics, Vitruvius recorded the use of **resonator vessels** in theaters: bronze or ceramic vessels were placed embedded under the bleachers to **absorb and equalize sound**, based on positioning calculations. That is, it was empirically recognized that air and neighboring materials (each space point) locally modulate sound propagation.

In Mesopotamia, cuneiform tuning texts (*CBS 10996* etc.) deal with pairs of neighboring strings (*"dichords"*). In them, fifths and fourths were tuned consecutively over adjacent strings, implying that **one string pair's interval is controlled by neighboring strings**. Modern studies show Mesopotamians tuned seven-string instruments via successive fifth and fourth cycles. This recursive model suggests each note is determined as a function of its immediate surroundings, analogous to a neighborhood operator.

- **Third rule in China:** In ancient Chinese music treatises (e.g., *Guanzi*, *Lüshi Chunqiu*), tube tuning followed schemes close to three-part division ("third rule"), so each tone derives from its neighbor. Although exact sources are complex, the general idea is the same: the next note is obtained from the previous one by a fixed fractional adjustment.

**Where does the ancient describe "what is beside determines the here"?** In all these ancient cases — Śulba-sūtra cords, Vitruvian acoustic couplings, Babylonian and Chinese tunings — each space/cord point (each "here") is seen to be defined by the state of adjacent points. This is a neighborhood intuition typical of continuous systems, long before the modern formulation of \(\nabla^2\).

## 3. \(\alpha(-\Delta)^{\sigma/2}\) — action at a distance, non-local
This term symbolizes long-range interactions — distant things influencing each other instantaneously, without contact. Ancient philosophies recognized similar principles of **"cosmic sympathy."** In Greece, the *harmony of the spheres* of Pythagoras/Plato held that celestial bodies emitted an invisible "sound" or "music" resonating through the cosmos. As the *Musica Universalis* encyclopedia says, Pythagoras proposed that Sun, Moon, and planets "each produce their hum" based on their orbits, though inaudible to ears. Plato saw astronomy and music as twin studies of numbers, and Aristotle even reports the Pythagorean thesis that star movement should create consonant sounds (with speed ratio equal to musical intervals).

In China, the doctrine of *Huangzhong* and the twelve lǜ centers on the fixed fundamental tone (黄钟) from which all derive. Per ancient legends, the Yellow Emperor asked Ling Lun to create bamboo tubes to discover the standard tone (Huangzhong) and, from it, defined the twelve musical modes. This model presupposes a **global resonant frame**: changing the fundamental tone (e.g., via the "chi" or Chinese foot used in the scale) changes the whole system. Jing Fang and related texts held that musical *qi* impregnates the soil and resonates with the seasons, suggesting a buried tube "responds" without direct mechanical contact.

Stoic and Hermetic philosophy also speaks of "sympathy": everything in the universe is interconnected by a common *pneuma*, so one event affects another instantaneously. By analogy, in a Tantric **yantra**, the bindu (central point) radiates to the whole sacred design at once, without explicit tracing of lines, representing "at a distance" action in the symbolic field.

**Which geometries require action at a distance?** Ancient views point to cosmos-wide integrated systems: the celestial spheres (universal harmony), the sun-axis in aligned temples (as in Karnak or Stonehenge, where sunlight and acoustic modes converge at one point) and Tantric mandalas, operating non-locally. In all of them, "action" (be it sound, light, or symbol) extends without immediate physical contact relative to the underlying geometric structure.

## 4. \(\Lambda|\Psi|^2\Psi\) — (nonlinear) self-reference
This term recalls "self-reflection" or "feedback" of the system upon itself. Various mystical traditions exhibit similar concepts of creative self-awareness. In Śaiva Trika, *vimarśa* denotes consciousness reflecting on itself: the inner light (*prakāśa*) that "knows itself" and is aware of itself. Analogously, *pāśyantī-vāk* (inner speech) is the level where thought's sound becomes visible to mind, like a sound that "sees itself."

Classic oracular phrases (e.g., Delphi's famous Γνῶθι σεαυτόν) already suggested the importance of self-knowledge as "knowing oneself." In the Jewish Kabbalah of **Sefer Yetzirah**, creation occurs through letters forming the divine Name, and that Name assumes and forms the letters themselves (a creative cycle of mutual identity). The Alexandrian *ouroboros* symbol — the serpent biting its own tail — visually expresses this creator-self-created unity. Plotinus (Enneads) describes the One (the Intellect) generating itself by contemplating itself infinitely, without a different exterior.

**Where is the creator principle at once the created and the observer?** In all these traditions, the creative agent coincides with creation perceiving itself. In short: the Light observing itself (vimarśa), the sound self-visualizing (paśyantī), the God forming His own Name are versions of the same self-referential phenomenon, analogous to the \(\Lambda|\Psi|^2\Psi\) term.

## 5. \(V_{\mathrm{mem}}\) — field memory
This term represents *trails of the past* remaining in the system (*samskāra*, *vāsanā*, etc.). For example, in Yoga and Vedānta philosophy, **saṃskāras** (mental impressions) and **vāsanās** (subtle tendencies) are marks left by past experiences, conditioning the present (Yoga Sūtra 2.12). Likewise, the Egyptian concept of **Maʽat** requires each action to be recorded in a perpetual cosmic order; every act must recall divine order. In the Vedic oral tradition, the hymns' very *meter* (*śruti*) is the texts' institutional memory: sacred rhythm is literally the vehicle transmitting content across generations (a "living text" never lost). Plato speaks of **anágnosis**/anamnesis (innate notions recalled by the soul) — memory of the prior condition. Ancient calendars (China, Mesopotamia) are also "rhythmic memories": the annual cycle institutionalizes when certain notes or festivals must occur, linking past and present musically.

**Which systems bring the past into the present's equation without being mere "history"?** Highly elaborated memory cultures serve as examples. The Vedas are "heard" (*śruti* means "that which is heard"), emphasizing a living recollection tradition. The Chinese agricultural calendar (81 moon/sun intervals) and Mesopotamian ziggurats serve as institutional memory of celestial and sonic cycles. In short, oral cults, repetition rituals, and calendar systems are ways ancient impressions (*saṃskāras*) participate in current reality, fulfilling the \(V_{\mathrm{mem}}\) role without reducing them to mere "history."

## 6. \(V_{\mathrm{ext}}\) — the exterior (the "outside")
This term corresponds to external forces or imponderable substrates. Various ancient cosmologies posit a **primordial chaos** (Apeiron, *Nūn*, *Pradhāna*, etc.) distinct from the inner active principle. In Egypt, *Nūn* personifies the chaotic primordial waters existing before and around creation. Plato (Timaeus 52–53) distinguishes the Artisan God (Demiurge) from formless receptive matter: the Demiurge imprints order on a pre-existing "receptacle" (the *khôra* or *apeíron*), a kind of neutral substrate where forms are born.

Culturally, this distinction appears in threshold rites: the circular furrow around the Egyptian temple, the cord delimiting the Vedic altar (sacred square) or the Tantric *bhūpūra* (yantra perimeter) separate "what vibrates" (the inner consecrated space) from "that in which it vibrates" (the outer profane soil). In short, many ancient systems saw **active reality vs. receptacle** as distinct concepts: inner sound, creator Word, or divine spirit versus the ether, chaos, or physical space receiving that vibration.

## 7. \(-i\Gamma\) — loss, dissipation, mode death
This term models energy dissipation. In cosmogonies, Vedic **sacrifices** illustrate this: ritual fire consumes offerings (energy) to maintain the world, sacrificing part of the real to sustain the cosmos. **Cyclic destruction** myths (Pralaya, Ragnarök, Egyptian Ogdoad) express the principle that forms persist only while there is constant "dripping" — without sacrifice (or energy leakage), nothing subsists. In ancient medicine, *humor* or *qi* levels travel with time: health follows continuous replenishment; its exhaustion brings disease (paralleling pulse damping). Even in Greek theater, it was observed that actors' sound died in the air if unconcentrated ("amplified" in Vitruvian fashion by vessels); each performance spends energy, like one "burning" sonic cord.

**Where does form permanence require leakage?** In all these examples: the Veda's living world requires periodic offerings (substance dissipation), the Greek soul tragedy's reprises (echo in the amphitheater), and mythic cycles assume inevitable end and rebirth. These evidence that any fixed order demands constant discharge or loss, metaphorized by the \(-i\Gamma\) term.

## 8. \(\eta\) — chance, residual noise
This term represents "chance forces" or non-deterministic residues. In ancient practice, unexplainable elements were attributed to chance or the system's "remainder." In the **Yìjīng (I Ching)**, for example, *Yì* (Change) incorporates luck/divination: hexagrams can be generated by coin or yarrow throws, giving room to the imponderable in human choices. In Pythagorean music, mathematicians had to deal with the *leimma* (remainder): the ∼1.0535 interval (256:243) left when dividing the tone (9:8), and the semitone (256:243) does not close in exact rational ratios. This "fractional" was seen as irrational, analogous to noise in a perfect scale. Acoustic experiments (Chladni, etc.) also show small impurities generate unexpected patterns on vibrating plates — cymatics itself defines a pattern as "imperfect." Ancient oracles and lots (dice, Roman lots, I Ching oracles) were precisely interpretations of this unpredictable remainder: system responses not following the obvious "law" or conscious will.

**Does the ancient system admit a term neither law nor will?** Yes: it appears as "fatum" or residual chaos in many myths. The *Yi Jing* accepts non-determinism through hexagrammatization; the Pythagoreans themselves described the inevitable leimma in their metric system. In rituals, the oracle is admitted to "read" what remains of the game. Thus, though there was no formal concept of stochastic noise, recognizing unexplained or impalpable remainders (unexplored *anāhata* nāda, emphasis on random transformation, etc.) demonstrates an awareness analogous to the \(\eta\) term.

## 9. Compared methodology (measures and ratios)
To understand these parallels, a **comparative metrology** is proposed: tables of length units and musical ratios in each culture. For example, checking whether the Egyptian cubit (~52.3 cm), the Vedic *puruṣa* (~1.8 m), the Chinese *chi* (~23.5 cm), or the Śulba cord form simple musical ratios (2:1, 3:2, 4:3, or the 256:243 leimma). Where these units relate length and tone, this suggests an ancestral geometric link: cultures using one standard length to build altars might also have used it for sound standards. Such inquiry would reveal whether concepts like "world navel" (scale root) appear in different places.

## 10. Archaeoacoustics with protocol
Fantasizing about "mystical" temples is not enough: one must measure. Archaeoacoustic studies at ancient sites (e.g., Karnak's inner chambers, Abydos, Khufu's pyramid) can determine local **resonance frequencies**, normal modes, reverberation times (RT60), etc. With these data, one examines whether there is coincidence with musical notes or the historical ritual's note. Sound dynamics in these enclosures (like "neighboring chambers" weakly coupling) is compared with \(\nabla^2\) predictions. Thus, the role of resonator vessels (per Vitruvius) and architectural contours as sound controllers is empirically tested, distinguishing practical effect from mystical overinterpretation.

## 11. Cymatics as controlled analogy
Standing-wave patterns (Chladni and Jenny figures) show frequency generates form. But this must be used as **controlled analogies**, not direct proofs. For example, if vibrated metal plates recreate mandala symmetries or six-pointed stars (Sri Yantra pillars or Pythagorean tetraktys), this corroborates an age-old nexus between sound and geometry. However, many modern patterns do not arise in harmonic vibration: pressing this would lead to erroneous conclusions. Therefore, rigorous criteria: only arrangements effectively repeated on vibrated blades should be associated with ancient structures, avoiding "forcing" unfounded connections.

## 12. Philology of the verb "to be"
Fundamental is separating literal from metaphorical claims. Example: "the world is sound" (*nāda brahma*, *spanda*) versus "the world is **like** sound" (late metaphor found in the Christian West). Only the first declares precise ontological identities, of interest to our equation; the second is mere analogy. In each ancient language, nuances should be examined: e.g., in Sanskrit *asti* and Hebrew *hayá* ("to be") may imply completeness versus mere resemblance. Specifically, one investigates whether Tantric texts insist *Jagat* **is** *nāda*, or say it seems to be. This philology avoids confusing romantic metaphor ("God is music") with exact doctrine compatible with the equation's terms.

## 13. History of the sound vs. geometry split
One maps when each tradition separated sound from geometry. The historical "split" occurs partly from Euclid (analytic geometric view) and 1834 (first use of the term *scientist*), when Christian mathematicians came to see music as mere art, not science. Before that, a unified approach (*ancient conjecture*) was common. From Helmholtz and non-Euclideans on, wave physics vs. mathematics drifted apart. Identifying these landmarks delimits which ancient knowledge still forms a harmonic whole (like Pythagoras) and which are later anachronistic projections.
