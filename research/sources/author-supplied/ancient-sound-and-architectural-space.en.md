The question asks what ancient history already recorded about sound — texts, instruments, myths, musical theory, ritual use, and the little the ancients themselves wrote about space — before the scientific field existed that measures the resonance of caves, dolmens, and temples. That cut matters: archaeoacoustics did not invent ancient sound; it invented a way of measuring the space the texts almost never describe.

What changes when the discipline is born

Until the end of the 20th century, the past was treated, in Paul Devereux's phrase, as a silent film. It was known there were flutes, lyres, hymns, and theaters. Nobody measured whether a megalithic chamber resonated at 110 Hz, whether Paleolithic paintings coincided with echoes, whether the Hal Saflieni hypogeum or the Chavín temple had been chosen for how the voice behaved there.

The new field's milestones are sharp:
- 1950s-60s: Bernard Fagg describes rock gongs in Nigeria; André Glory notes struck stalactites in French caves with rock art;
- 1983-88: Iégor Reznikoff and Michel Dauvois sing in Le Portel, Niaux, and Arcy-sur-Cure and correlate resonance with painting placement;
- 2003: Cambridge symposium (Chris Scarre and Graeme Lawson) names the field.

In Neolithic China, Jiahu (Henan) delivered playable crane-bone flutes with 5 to 8 holes, 9 to 7 thousand years old. They are the oldest multi-note instruments that still sound. In Neolithic Europe and the Near East appear ceramic rattles, conchs used as trumpets, scrapers, bullroarers. In the Bronze Age, metals enter: bells, cymbals, horns.

The Divje Babe (Slovenia) "Neanderthal flute" remains contested: most specialists read the holes as carnivore tooth marks. The sound cut begins with the European Aurignacian and the Chinese Neolithic.

Mesopotamia: the world's first musical theory

Here the written archive explodes. It is the only place, besides Greece, where notation survived.

Around 2600 BC, Ur's Royal Cemetery buries musicians with their lyres. The bull lyres — bovine-body-shaped soundbox, gold and lapis-lazuli head — are not isolated pieces: there are eight lyres, harps, a silver double flute, sistrum, cymbals. The complexity presupposes centuries of development the soil did not preserve.

Enheduanna, daughter of Sargon of Akkad, high priestess at Ur (~2300 BC), is the first composer of known name. The hymn texts to Inanna and Nanna survived — not the melody.

Around 1800 BC the Babylonians stop transmitting by ear alone and write theory. Four tablets are the core:
- UET VII 126: nine named lyre strings (from "front" to "back"; the fourth is "Ea made");
- UET VII 74: tuning instructions;
- CBS 10996 and CBS 1766: intervals.

There are seven named tunings (isartum, kitmum, embubum, pitum, nid qablim, [remainder lost in conversion] years before Pythagoras. The Palace of Mari, destroyed by Hammurabi in 1759 BC, left about 20 thousand tablets: musicians are royal functionaries, teach the court's young women, play in cult, feasts, and war's return.

The most famous sound document is the Hurrian Hymn to Nikkal (h.6 from Ugarit, ~1400-1250 BC): Hurrian text, Akkadian interval rubrics for a nine-string lyre [sentence fragment lost in conversion] can tentatively be reconstructed. Reconstructions diverge — Kilmer, Dumbrill, West disagree on absolute pitch — but the fact of notation is indisputable. Mesopotamia and [sentence end lost in conversion].

What does not exist: a Mesopotamian treatise on how a temple should resonate. There is tuning theory, not room theory.

Egypt: sound as theology, silence as virtue

Egypt left no notation. It left the Mediterranean's densest iconographic archive and an explicit theology of sacred noise.

Material chronology:
- 5th millennium BC: rattles in sanctuaries and tombs;
- 4th millennium: clappers on vessels; flute on the Two Dogs Palette (Hierakonpolis);
- Old Kingdom (~2575-2134 BC): paintings with harps, oblique flutes, single and double reeds;
- Middle Kingdom onward: lutes, drums, mixed ensembles.

The theological instrument is the sistrum — naos (ssst / sesheshet) and arched (shm / [fragment lost in conversion] of Music; the son Ihy holds sistrum and menat. The Dendera hymns are literal: the sound "gladdens the goddess's heart" and "drives away the Eye of Ra's fury". The dry noise of the rods [fragment lost in conversion] …child was hidden. Osiris, in the myth, civilizes humanity with Hathor's music.

The Sistrum of Ancient Egypt: Sacred Rattle of the Gods

The other pole, less cited and just as important, is silence. The Instruction of Ani: do not raise the voice in the god's house — he abhors shouts; pray with loving heart and hidden words. Edfu asks that nobody drown another's voice. Amenemope praises the silent one in the temple. Ptah "hears prayers"; ears sculpted on walls are not decorative metaphor. Ritual sound is dosage: the sistrum is pharmacopeia against divine wrath; the profane shout is offense.

No Egyptian text exists saying "we designed the hypostyle for such frequency". That is modern inference. What the Egyptians stated: the right sound pleases the god; the wrong noise repels him.

Levant and Israel: theophany as sound event

The biblical inventory is organological and liturgical, not acoustic-architectural. Instruments: shofar (most cited, ~70 times), silver hasoserot (Nm 10), kinnor (David's lyre), nevel, tof, metsiltayim, halil, ugav. The shofar announces jubilee, war, the Sinai presence. Exodus 19 describes the theophany as crescendo: thunders, the ever-louder shofar, the mountain trembles. Sound is the presence. 2 Chronicles 5, at the Temple's dedication, joins 120 priests with trumpets and the Levites "as one voice" and the glory fills the house. 1 Chronicles 25 links prophecy to harps, nevels, and cymbals. The Mishnah preserves the daily choreography: trumpets, Ben Arza's cymbal, the psalm of the day, the people prostrating at each section. Psalm 150 is an instrument catalogue. Jericho (Jo 6) is theological narrative, not an acoustic-engineering manual. Kenyon's archaeology does not confirm a wall contemporary with the narrated conquest. What was known: the Temple had a trained choir, strictly ritual-function instruments, and the collective voice was conceived as the presence's vehicle. What was not known: the Holy of Holies' reverberation time.

India: the Absolute as sound

The Indian tradition goes furthest in sound metaphysics and earliest classifies instruments by acoustic principle, not material. The Vedas call themselves sruti: "what is heard". The Samaveda is the Veda of chant: udgatr in the soma sacrifice. Memorization includes up to eleven recitation forms of the same text. Living continuity: Vedic chant is still transmitted.

Underneath lies an ontology: Nada-Brahman / Sabda-Brahman — the Absolute [fragment lost in conversion] pasyanti, madhyama, vaikhari). Ahata nada is struck sound; anahata nada is the cosmos's "unstruck" sound. OM/pranava is the primordial sound. The Natyasastra (36.27) equates vocal and instrumental music with Vedic mantra recitation.

The technical treatise is Bharata's Natyasastra (~200 BC-200 AD). Chapters 28-34: four instrument classes by what vibrates — tata (string), susira (air column), avanaddha (membrane), ghana (solid body). It is the direct ancestor, two thousand years earlier, of the Hornbostel-Sachs system;
- the octave divided into 22 srutis;
- empirical method: two equal vinas; one string is loosened until the ear perceives two notes — discrimination threshold, not numerology.

Later temples will have kuttampalam and performance halls per the treatise's geometry. That is stage-and-rite prescription, not reverberation measurement. Hindu temples' acoustics as a physics object is, again, a modern question.

China: tone as State standard

Ancient China treats pitch as a weights-and-measures problem, not only aesthetics. The legend: the Yellow Emperor sends Ling Lun to Kunlun to cut bamboo. The first tube is huangzhong 黄钟, "yellow bell", cosmic fundamental. The other eleven lü 律 are born by the sanfen sunyi 三分损益 method: a third of the length is removed or added — 2:3 and 3:4 ratios, the same fifth cycle the Pythagoreans formalize on the other side of the continent, independently. The system is in Lushi chunqiu (~239 BC) and Sima Qian's Shiji.

Consequences Greece lacks with the same intensity:
- the standard tube enters the state metrology office;
- the twelve lü correlate with months, earthly branches, yin/yang;
- Jing Fang (78-37 BC) perceives the 12-cycle does not close the octave (the comma) and proposes 60 standards;
- the 65-bell bianzhong set of Marquis Yi of Zeng (433 BC) — each bell with two tones, per strike point — is material evidence of sophisticated theory centuries before Vitruvius.

Needham noted the style difference: Chinese acoustics is "pneumatic" [contrast lost in conversion]. Two physics, one problem: how to generate and standardize pitch.

Greece and Rome: the first physics of sound — and the only explicit acoustic design

Here, for the first time in the West, sound becomes a natural-philosophy and engineering object.

Theory. To the Pythagoreans (6th-5th c. BC) is attributed the discovery of 2:1 (octave), 3:2 (fifth), 4:3 (fourth) ratios on the monochord. The forge anecdote (Nicomachus, Iamblichus, Boethius) is late and physically false: hammer weight is not string tension. What is solid: Philolaus already describes the scale; Archytas of Tarentum (fr. 1 Huffman) defines sound as traveling impact (plege); blow force would explain loudness and pitch — the two magnitudes were separated later. Aristotle gets air propagation right and errs saying sharp tones travel faster. Aristoxenus (4th c. BC), in Elementa harmonica, inverts the criterion: the ear, not reason alone, judges the interval; tone is divisible in perception. Ptolemy, Harmonica (2nd c. AD), attempts the synthesis. The pseudo-Aristotelian Problemata already describe resonance and echo. Musical ethos — the Doric mode educates, the Phrygian excites — is Plato's and Aristotle's doctrine, not room acoustics.

Architecture. Vitruvius, De architectura V.3-5 (c. 30-15 BC), is the ancient text closest to an applied-acoustics treatise. Voice is "flowing breath", like waves from a stone in water, but in all directions. The theater site cannot be "deaf"; the gradation section must follow the rising voice's line. And then the echeia / echea: bronze vessels (or, in poor theaters, clay) tuned in fourths, fifths, up to the double octave, housed in niches under the cavea, not touching the wall, with slits in the seats below. The stage voice, departing from center, "strikes the cavities and wakes a note in unison with itself". It is a Helmholtz resonator described eighteen centuries before Helmholtz. Vitruvius says Lucius Mummius took the bronze vessels from Corinth's theater to Rome and dedicated them in the Moon's temple. Nearly a thousand Greco-Roman theaters are known; Epidaurus, by Polykleitos the Younger (4th c. BC), is the paradigm of speech intelligibility at tens of meters.

The North: lurs, no treatise

Bronze Age Scandinavia (~1300-500 BC) leaves about 60 bronze lurs — S-tubes, 1.5 to 2.5 m, bell with ornamented disc, mouthpiece [fragment lost in conversion] as offering. Tanum and Bardal rock art shows paired players by boats. Playable lurs today emit 8 to 12 natural harmonics. They are not Vikings: the birch-bark lur (neverlur) and the wooden one are medieval. Oseberg (834) has a case, not the bronze instrument.

There is no pre-Christian Germanic acoustic treatise. What there is:

The bronze lurs' most plausible function, by deposition context and solar iconography, is cycle ritual — not "court orchestra".

Mesoamerica and Andes: instruments and chronicles, no written physics

Before the conquest, the archive is material and pictorial. After, chronicles. Mesoamerica: teponaztli (slit drum with two tongues, two tones), huehuetl (vertical jaguar-skin drum), quiquiztli / atecocoli conch (announces cult hours; Ehécatl-Quetzalcoatl's creator breath), multiple Teotihuacan flutes, zoomorphic ocarinas, long wooden trumpets in the Bonampak murals (791 AD) — twelve-piece orchestra in file: rattles, tall drum, tortoise shells, trumpets. The Florentine Codex (Sahagún) describes the musical storehouse (mixcoacall) and conch use in the four directions. Bernal Díaz hears, terrified, the drums in the Templo Mayor during sacrifices. Andes: quena (bone since the first millennium BC), siku / antara, pututu (conch), tinya. Garcilaso de la Vega (1609) describes cane tubes in four voices among the Collas and ritual roar in moon eclipses. Betanzos (1551) speaks of four gold drums in the plaza and collective song. Reading Guaman Poma as "Inca acoustic theory" (separate places for pinkullu and harawi) is fragile: the text speaks of named places, not room modes. Stevenson (1968) exaggerated; Stobart corrected. The serpent echo on Kukulkan's stairway, pututu resonance in Chavin de Huantar, Teotihuacan's sound map — that is 20th-21st-century archaeoacoustics. It was not in the chronicles.

What the ancients already said about space — and the hard limit

Joining only what they stated about sound's place, the inventory is short and uneven:

| Culture | What the text/practice states | What it does not state |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mesopotamia | Tuning, modes, palatial and cultual function | Ziggurat resonance design |
| Egypt | Sistrum calms Hathor; silence in the temple | Hypostyle frequency |
| Israel | One voice + trumpets = glory in the Temple | Debir reverberation |
| India | Sound = Brahman; 22 srutis; 4 vibration classes | Garbhagrha measurement |
| China | Pitch = cosmic and state standard | Palace acoustics as room |
| Greece/Rome | Voice as wave; tuned vessels in the theater; "non-deaf" site | Measurement in decibels or Hz |
| North | Paired lur, offering, rite | Theory |
| Mesoamerica | Conch in the directions; dual drum | Pyramid's designed echo |

Vitruvius is the exception: he describes a resonator device with tuning and position. The rest of the ancient world speaks of instrument, rite, myth, and tuning. Almost never of room as instrument.

Three limits run through all this.
1. Melody is lost. Enheduanna, the Hurrian hymns, the Samaveda, the Psalms: we have text, sometimes interval cipher, almost never the sound. Reconstructions are hypotheses.
2. Pitch theory ≠ space acoustics. Babylon, China, and Greece solve "how to generate the fifth". Only Hellenistic/Roman Greece writes "how the voice fills the theater".
3. Prehistoric intentionality is inference. That Hohle Fels has a flute does not say whether the chamber was chosen for echo. That question is exactly what archaeoacoustics formulates — and what ancient sources, by definition, do not measure.

The new discipline does not replace that corpus. It adds what the corpus could not give: the number. Reverberation times, chambers' eigenmodes (~95-120 Hz in several European megaliths), painting-resonance correlation, Epidaurus digital simulation, Chichen's "quetzal", pututus in Andean galleries. Without the texts and instruments above, those numbers would have nothing to mean. Without the numbers, the texts stay mute about the air around them.
