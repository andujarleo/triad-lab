# From Spanda to the Yijing: Mapping Cosmic Origin, Order and Destiny in Ancient Cosmologies through Analogies with the Quantum Equation

## The Pulse as Origin: Spanda, Vāc and the Creator Logos

The first philosophical question associated with the $i\hbar\partial_t\Psi$ term of the quantum equation — "Is the 'before the world' silence or is it vibration still without an object?" — serves as a fundamental starting point for the investigation, since it seeks the primordial origin, the originary activity before differentiated manifestation. This section analyzes how different ancient traditions conceptualized this initial pulse, distinguishing between models of self-generation, creation from a pre-existing substrate and emergence from a mathematical cut. The most direct correspondences are found in Indian Trika Śaivism, in the Egyptian theology of Memphis and in the Genesis myth, offering fertile ground for analogical comparison. The crucial methodological distinction between ontological identity ("the world *is* sound") and metaphor ("the world *is like* sound"), as requested, will be rigorously maintained.

At the heart of the Indian Śaiva Nondualist philosophy (Trika), we find the notion of **Spanda**, which corresponds remarkably to the idea of an originary pulse. Spanda is not understood as a physical or mechanical movement, but as the primordial pulsation of consciousness itself (caitanya), the originary activity of Śiva. This activity does not precede consciousness; it *is* consciousness itself, a state of dynamic self-experience. Kṣemarāja's commentary on the Spanda Kārikās explores this nuance, stating that Spanda is the manifestation of the vibratory energy of absolute reality. This conception aligns directly with the $i\hbar\partial_t\Psi$ term, where the temporal operator ($\partial_t$) implies an intrinsic and continuous change, a "pulse" that defines the evolution of the quantum state $\Psi$. Spanda, therefore, does not arise *from* something; it *is* the first and immanent cause of all existence. Other Vedic sources reinforce this view. The Ṛgveda, in its Hymn to Vāc (Ṛgveda X.125), personifies the Divine Word as the creating force that establishes cosmic order, declaring that "ela rouse e ordene a batalha para o povo" [garbled in source] and "ela bendita a arco para Rudra" [garbled in source] [[661](https://www.academia.edu/43684336/%E1%B9%9A%E1%B9%A3is_the_Poets_of_the_%E1%B9%9Agveda), [662](https://www.academia.edu/118705592/Language_and_Reality_in_Indian_Tradition), [664](https://www.academia.edu/46863190/Puranas_Collection_of_Essays_on_Hindu_Temples)]. Although Vāc is often translated as "word", its meaning is deeper, encompassing sound, vibration and the creative principle underlying the universe [[5](https://philarchive.org/archive/MASTNA-6), [160](https://iep.utm.edu/upanisad/)]. Likewise, the Nāda-Bindu Upaniṣad and Hamsa Upaniṣad explicitly distinguish between *nāda anāhata*, the unstruck, inaudible and unmanifest sound, and manifested sound (*nāda ahata*). *Nāda anāhata* represents that primordial vibration, the latent potential before sonic creation, aligning perfectly with the idea of a pulse without a defined object.

In contrast, Egyptian cosmogony presents an interesting complexity. The central Heliopolitan myth, derived from the Pyramid Texts, narrates creation by the deity Atum, who emerges alone from primordial chaos, the infinite ocean of Nun [[188](https://www.facebook.com/groups/498345872555514/posts/933589372364493/), [315](https://study.com/academy/lesson/video/ancient-egyptian-god-atum-creation-myth-appearance-facts.html)]. Atum is described as the "self-created god", arising from "nothingness" or "nun" [[28](https://www.facebook.com/61574974992206/posts/%EF%B8%8F-atum-the-self-created-god-of-ancient-egyptin-ancient-egyptian-cosmology-atum-w/122163957428832499/), [191](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vcr1_mRqLn0)]. He engenders the first gods, Shu and Tefnut, from himself, whether through his saliva or by masturbation [[317](https://religion.fandom.com/wiki/Atum)]. This narrative suggests that the origin is not a simple silence or vibration, but the emergence of an ordered form from a formless and pre-existing substrate of water and darkness [[124](https://www.facebook.com/groups/2184643541565906/posts/27542541052016139/), [130](https://www.ancient-origins.net/news-human-origins/egyptian-creation-myth-0021331)]. Thus, the distinction between "what vibrates" and "that in which it vibrates" is explicit from the start: Nun is the field of unmanifest potentialities, and Atum is the ordered manifestation within it. However, the Theology of Memphis, recorded on the Shabaka Stone, offers a different perspective, more aligned with the logocentric creation model [[68](http://www.sofiatopia.org/maat/memphis.htm), [73](https://www.academia.edu/95147693/New_findings_about_the_Memphite_Theology)]. In this myth, the god Ptah creates the cosmos not through a physical act, but through the power of thought ("heart") and word ("tongue") [[477](https://henadology.wordpress.com/theology/netjeru/ptah/), [590](http://www.sofiatopia.org/maat/verb.htm), [591](https://www.facebook.com/Egyptouring/posts/ptah-is-the-most-important-god-of-men-nefer-memphis-although-not-the-oldest-beca/10158721669479541/)]. Ptah designs all other divinities and forms in his heart and vocalizes them with his tongue, becoming the invisible artisan who shapes the universe through linguistic intentionality [[481](https://www.facebook.com/Egypt.Culture/posts/in-egyptian-mythology-ptah-is-the-demiurge-of-memphis-god-of-craftsmen-and-archi/1450266395023206/)]. This triad — thought (intention), tongue (expression) and creation (result) — works as a precise model for the generation of an initial state $\Psi$ from a self-reflexive consciousness. The phrase "Ptah created everything" is emphasized, positioning him as the ultimate cause of all existence [[590](http://www.sofiatopia.org/maat/verb.htm)].

The cosmogony of the Hebrew Levant, particularly in Genesis 1, also operates on the basis of a creator Logos. God creates the world through speech: "And God said..." It is an act of divine decree that brings light, the heavens, the earth and all living beings into existence [[1](https://www.cs.williams.edu/~lindsey/myths/myths_15.html), [198](https://biblicalstudies.in/index.php/pub/article/download/30/23)]. Similarly, the Sefer Yetzirah (Kaplan Edition, comparing recensions) details the creation of the world from the letters of the Hebrew alphabet, which are combined to form the divine names and the structures of the universe. This is a form of textual self-reference: the name is made of the letters it combines, suggesting a circularity between the symbol and what it represents. Philosophers such as Philo of Alexandria interpret the Logos as the ordering principle of nature, a cut and measure that introduces reason into chaos. This model contrasts with Spanda, since creation is not self-activity, but an external act of a demiurge.

Finally, the Pythagorean school, through the fragments of Philolaus, offers a purely mathematical structural analogy for the birth of reality. He postulates two primordial principles: the Limiting (peras) and the Unlimited (apeiron). Reality emerges from the interaction and the cut (*logos*) between these two principles. The Limiting is what confers form, number and structure; the Unlimited is raw matter, multiplicity and infinite potential. The harmony and order of the cosmos result from this dialectical relation. This idea can be seen as a model of extreme local coupling, where structure emerges from the neighborhood of a defined limit. In short, the analysis of the originary pulse reveals three distinct models:
1.  **Self-generation (Consciousness-Activity):** Represented by Spanda, where the origin is the very activity of consciousness (Śiva). The cause is internal and immanent.
2.  **Creation From a Substrate (Intentional Logos):** Represented by Ptah and Genesis, where an external consciousness (or an intention) acts upon pre-existing material (Nun, chaos).
3.  **Emergent Structure (Mathematical Cut):** Represented by Philolaus, where reality emerges from the relation and the cut between abstract principles.

This preliminary analysis demonstrates that, although cultural expressions vary enormously, ancient traditions developed sophisticated conceptual structures to deal with the question of origin, often using analogies of sound, word and proportion. Subsequent investigation of the other terms of the equation will seek to see how these origin models develop and interact within their respective cosmologies.

## Local and Non-Local Coupling: Strings, Resonance and Universal Harmony

The second philosophical question, "where does the ancient describe 'what is beside determines the here'?", points to coupling mechanisms, both local and non-local, that govern the structure and dynamics of reality. This section examines how diverse ancient civilizations conceptualized neighborhood relations and long-distance connections, using analogies of musical strings, bronze vessels, scales of notes and ritual geometries. The correspondence with the $-\hbar^2/2m\nabla^2$ term, which describes diffusion or propagation in a continuum, and with $\alpha(-\Delta)^{\sigma/2}$, which models non-local interactions, is central in this analysis.

For local coupling, the **Śulba Sūtras** of Ancient India provide a paradigmatic example. These Vedic texts, which deal with geometry for the construction of altars and temples, use a cord as an operational tool to measure and trace lines, angles and areas. The cord acts as a mediator of point-to-point relations, transforming complex geometric problems into sequences of local and contiguous measurements. This reflects a "local coupling" logic: the global form is built from a series of local relations defined by the cord. Similarly, in Ancient Greece, the architect Vitruvius, in his work *De Architectura*, describes the use of bronze vessels suspended under the arcades of theaters to improve acoustics. The purpose was to make the air, acting as a continuous medium, propagate the orator's sound more clearly and prominently. Here, coupling is mediated by the medium itself (air), where the disturbance at one point (the orator's voice) propagates and influences adjacent points. In Mesopotamia, the clay tablet CBS 10996, a text on the tuning of musical instruments, shows that the musicians and theorists of the time had a sophisticated knowledge of harmonic intervals, such as the fifth (3:2) and the fourth (4:3), which represent neighborhood relations on the scale of a vibrating string. These relations define how a note is connected to its immediate neighbors. In China, music treatises such as the *Lüshu* (Shiji) and the *Guanzi* (Yin-Yang chapter) discuss the "rule of thirds" (*lü*) for calculating the positions of tones on a scale, again defining the musical "immediate neighbor". The correspondence with the equation is as follows: the Laplacian ($\nabla^2$) mathematically captures diffusion in a continuum, while the ancient sources translate this idea into a network of discrete relations — whether a cord, a musical scale or a building plan.

The question of non-local coupling, on the other hand, raises the question of how distant parts of the cosmos may be interconnected. The most influential answer in this area was the **Harmony of the Spheres**, a doctrine that spread from Pythagoras to Iamblichus, Ptolemy and finally Kepler. The central idea was that the orbital spheres of the planets produce perfect sounds in their orbits, and the relations between these sounds are governed by simple mathematical proportions. Although the sound is not audible, universal harmony exists as an underlying mathematical structure that unites the whole cosmos. This is the ideal analogy for the $\alpha(-\Delta)^{\sigma/2}$ term, which describes a long-range interaction dependent on the geometry and topology of space. The correspondence with the Harmony of the Spheres suggests that some ancient civilizations had a conception of a "unifying field" (harmony) that mediated non-local relations through universal mathematical laws.

Another strong candidate for non-local coupling is the Chinese theory of **Huangzhong**. According to Jing Fang, tubes of specific bore were buried vertically in different regions of China. The tube buried at the center of the country (Huangzhong, the "central yellow tube") was said to respond to the vital Qi current of the specific season of the year, while the other tubes responded to the corresponding currents of their respective regions. This response occurred without any physical contact, indicating a form of resonance with a broader cosmic field. This evokes the idea of an energetic field (Qi) that permeates the universe and whose fluctuations manifest synchronously at distant points. Finally, the Hindu **Yantras**, especially the Śrī Yantra, offer a powerful geometric analogy. This sacred diagram is built from intersections of triangles and concentric circles, culminating in a central point called **bindu**. The bindu is considered the point of origin of all manifestation and exerts an influence over the entire geometric enclosure, not via propagation, but by an instantaneous and non-local action. It acts as a generating center of energy that sustains the entire structure, functioning as an analogy for a point field or a potential that defines the totality of the system.

Comparative metrology emerges as a crucial analytical tool in this domain. The question of whether the unit of length (such as the sacred cubit, the Greek foot or the Śulba cord) was also a unit of pitch is fundamental. If so, this would imply a fundamental dimensional equivalence between space and sound, suggesting that architecture and music were two faces of the same science of proportion. Concrete projects, such as the Ratio Sheet, which extracts the principal musical ratios (2:1, 3:2, 4:3, 9:8) and their "remainders" (leimma) from different cultures, may reveal patterns of preference or shared mathematical limitations. For example, McClain's thesis on the connection between the numbers of the gods in Babylonia and musical ratios could be tested and refined.

Archaeoacoustics offers a path to empirically validate these analogies. By measuring the natural resonance modes of underground chambers, such as those of the Ħal Saflieni Hypogeum or Newgrange, researchers can verify whether certain frequencies are amplified [[18](https://otsf.org/archaeoacoustics), [483](https://www.ctm-festival.de/magazine/sound-and-ancient-sacred-places)]. If a specific acoustic mode coincides with a solar alignment, as in the case of Karnak, this could indicate a ritual and geometric "action at a distance", where solar energy (light) and sound energy (sound) are synchronized at a focal point [[74](https://www.scienceopen.com/hosted-document?doi=10.14236/ewic/EVA2023.47)]. Studies have shown that chambers such as the Oracle Chamber in the Hypogeum resonate strongly around 110 Hz, a frequency that may induce altered states of consciousness [[415](https://www.facebook.com/TheAncientLibrary/posts/some-rooms-were-designed-to-resonatethe-%C4%A7al-saflieni-hypogeum-in-malta-carved-ar/1481108516974861/), [511](https://www.academia.edu/67552976/Archaeoacoustics_Physiological_Effects_of_Sound_in_Ancient_Megaliths)]. This empirical research transforms the discussion from a purely conceptual analogy to a possible physical correspondence, significantly enriching the comparative analysis.

## Self-Reference and Memory: The Conscious Field and Karmic Impressions

The philosophical questions associated with the $\Lambda|\Psi|^2\Psi$ terms ("Where is the creating principle at once what is created and what observes itself?") and $V_{\mathrm{mem}}$ ("Which systems make the past enter the equation of the present without being mere 'history'?") explore the reflexivity and remembrance dimensions of reality. This section analyzes how the Indian traditions, in particular Trika Śaivism and Vedanta, developed sophisticated concepts of self-perception and ontological memory, using analogies of self-reflexive consciousness and karmic impressions that survive the event. These ideas offer notable parallels for non-linear self-interaction and for the notion of a field with memory, which are fundamental features of certain formulations of quantum mechanics.

The concept of **Vimarśa**, central in Trika Śaivism, is the most precise correspondence for the self-reference described by the $\Lambda|\Psi|^2\Psi$ term. Vimarśa is the capacity of pure consciousness (Purusha) to reflect itself, to recognize itself. It is not a perception of an external object, but self-perception, the light that knows itself. While consciousness is the subject, the activity of perception is its own manifestation. This self-reflection is the process that generates phenomenal diversity from non-dual unity. The entire universe is seen as the manifestation of consciousness itself contemplating itself. The Alexandrian Ouroboros, the serpent eating its own tail, is a powerful visual symbol of this closed cycle of self-reference. Likewise, Plotinus's Ennead VI 9 describes the Intellect (*Nous*) as that which thinks itself, being thought and the object of thought simultaneously. In classical Islam, Ibn 'Arabi, in his doctrine of the "Unity of Being" (*Wahdat al-Wujud*), proposes that all manifestation is an expression of divine existence itself, creating a deeply self-contained ontological structure [[387](https://itsfilipholm.substack.com/p/what-is-sufi-music), [656](http://repository.dharmawangsa.ac.id/783/2/79.pdf)]. The $\Lambda|\Psi|^2\Psi$ term, with its non-linear form, captures precisely this idea that the state of the system ($\Psi$) dynamically influences its own evolution. It is a positive-feedback term, where the presence of the system amplifies its own presence, analogous to Vimarśa generating multiplicity from conscious singularity.

The memory of the field, represented by $V_{\mathrm{mem}}$, has its clearest counterpart in the concept of **Saṃskāra** (impressions) and **vāsanā** (residual tendencies) found in the texts of the Yoga-Sūtra and Vedanta. A Saṃskāra is an impression left by an experience that survives the original event, stored in the field of consciousness and capable of influencing future experiences and behaviors. It is a "memory" at the level of consciousness itself. This memory is not passive; it carries potentiality and acts as a predisposition. When a new experience occurs, it does not happen in a vacuum, but interacts with the field of accumulated Saṃskāras, which explains why different individuals react differently to the same stimuli. This is a direct analogy for a memory potential that makes the past "enter the equation of the present". The Egyptian Law of **Maat**, meaning order, truth and justice, imposes a cosmic standard that the ritual act must remember and re-encounter. The act does not create order from scratch, but restores it, adjusting the present to the eternal pattern of Maat. It is an institutional and cosmic memory.

The Vedic oral tradition offers another fascinating perspective. In the Veda, **meter** (poetic rhythm) is considered the memory that preserves the sacred text through the generations. The structural form (meter) carries the content (mantra), guaranteeing its integrity. Memory resides not only in meaning, but in the very vibratory structure of sound. This suggests that memory may be an intrinsic property of wave form. Calendars, used in both China and Mesopotamia, can be seen as institutional memory machines. The calendar is not merely a record of time, but a device that periodically reaffirms the initial configuration of the cosmos, the original "tuning", linking the present to the cosmic past in a renewable cycle.

In the Buddhist Yogācāra philosophy (also known as Vijnanavada), the concept of **Ālaya-vijñāna** (storehouse consciousness) plays a role similar to Saṃskāra, but on a more macrocosmic scale [[641](https://www.lionsroar.com/dharma-dictionary-yogacara/), [689](https://www.britannica.com/topic/alaya-vijnana)]. It is the fundamental consciousness that stores all karmic tendencies and impressions of all beings, functioning as a cosmic substrate for memory and renewal [[691](https://discourse.suttacentral.net/t/alaya-vijnana-and-early-buddhism/31403), [692](https://www.dharmawheel.net/viewtopic.php?t=40093)]. Although it is a Buddhist and not a Vedantin conception, its structure of a fundamental memory repository is conceptually close. However, it is important to note that, in Advaita Vedanta, ultimate reality is Brahman, which is timeless and absolutely real, while the phenomenal world, including the causal chain of Saṃskāras, is considered mithya (dependent or "without-truth") [[117](https://elisafreschi.com/2017/10/20/does-anything-exist-according-to-advaita-vedanta/), [263](https://www.thebrokentusk.com/post/anirvachaniya-the-indescribable-status-of-the-world)]. Ultimate reality is beyond any memory or remembrance, but the illusion of memory is the engine of the cycle of birth and death (*samsara*) [[453](https://philosophy.institute/indian-philosophy/avidya-maya-veils-ignorance-illusion-advaita/)].

The distinction between ontological identity and metaphor continues to be a challenge. Expressions such as "Sound is God" (*Nada Brahma*), which appear in Vedic contexts, indicate a radical ontological identification between the universe and sound/divinity [[153](https://www.overgrownpath.com/2014/09/nada-brahma-sound-is-god.html), [154](https://ravensounds.com/blogs/news/nada-brahma-the-ancient-truth-that-sound-is-the-universe?srsltid=AfmBOorFeOoxikr6cSFJIivuMzH8brMDCq8ItpMcMmAQqgrrsz33zHGF)]. The analysis must therefore be careful not to project the quantum structure onto analogies that are merely poetic. The correspondence with Saṃskāra and Maat seems more robust, since it describes functional mechanisms of information and influence that transcend mere poetry. The investigation of archaeoacoustics may also be applied here: if certain resonance frequencies in sacred spaces are consistently repeated in specific rituals, they could function as a sonic "memory", echoing the ritual past in the physical present.

## Boundaries and Dissipation: The Demiurge, Chaos and Ritual Sacrifice

The philosophical question for the $V_{\mathrm{ext}}$ term, "Does the ancient distinguish 'what vibrates' from 'that in which it vibrates'?", introduces the notion of boundaries, external environments and boundary conditions. The question for the $-i\Gamma$ term, "Where does the permanence of form require leakage?", addresses the need for dissipation and loss for the maintenance of order. This section explores how ancient cosmologies conceptualized the relation between the ordered world and its chaotic environment, and how deliberate loss (dissipation) is an essential component of life and creation.

The distinction between the system and its environment is most explicitly formulated in Plato's cosmology in the *Timaeus*. The Timaeus distinguishes between the **Demiurge**, the benevolent intelligence that shapes raw matter, and the **Receptacle** (*hypokeimenon*), the formless and passive matter that receives forms. The Receptacle is the "that in which" things are made, a substrate that presses upon and delimits creation. This dichotomy between the creative artisan and raw material is a powerful structural analogy for the $V_{\mathrm{ext}}$ term, which represents an external potential or a boundary influencing the dynamics of the internal system ($\Psi$). In Ancient Egypt, primordial chaos **Nun** plays a similar role. Nun is the primordial ocean from which the earth emerged and which contains the potential for creation, but also represents the threat of regression to chaos [[127](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nun_(mythology)), [128](https://www.facebook.com/groups/791402735327666/posts/1688120552322542/)]. Order (*Maat*) is constantly defended against the chaos of Nun. Likewise, in Hinduism, the concept of **Pradhāna**, unordered raw matter, and Anaximander's **Apeiron** (the Indefinite), represent the chaotic and unlimited substrate pressing upon the field of manifestation. Delimitation rituals, such as marking the furrow for the foundation of an Egyptian temple or demarcating the **bhūpura** (earth plan) of a Hindu yantra, were concrete practices for creating a physical and sacred boundary between the ordered world (inside) and the chaotic (outside). This boundary is not a void, but a threshold defining the identity of sacred space.

The $-i\Gamma$ term, representing loss, openness and dissolution, finds its strongest correspondences in concepts of ritual dissipation and destruction-recreation cycles. In the Veda, sacrifice (*yajna*) is a central act of controlled dissipation. The sacrificed offering is consumed by fire, transforming dense matter into subtle energy. This intentional loss does not eliminate order, but feeds and maintains it, transferring energy to the divine cosmos. Cosmic order depends on a constant flow of dissipation. This idea of "permanence of form requiring leakage" is central. The concept of **Enantiodromia**, the tendency of something to transform into its opposite, is a recurring theme. In the Hindu cosmic cycles of **Pralaya** (periodic dissolutions), the universe is destroyed to allow a new creation. Likewise, the Norse myths of Ragnarök, although from another tradition, illustrate the same principle of destruction and rebirth. This dissipation is not seen as an end, but as a necessary component of the cycle of life. In ancient medicine, the theory of humors and of depleting Qi also fits this category, where health depends on the balance and flow of vital fluids, and blockage leads to disease. The Greek theater, where sound needs the theater space (the "vessel") to be heard, is a powerful poetic analogy: form (sound) requires a void (environment) to exist and be perceived.

In Islamic philosophy, the influence of Neoplatonism is evident. Al-Farabi and Ibn Sina expanded the idea of **emanation**, where the One (God) radiates reality in a hierarchical cascade of beings, each less perfect than the previous one [[519](https://www.academia.edu/16651635/Emanation_and_its_impact_on_Muslim_philosophy)]. This emission is a form of "leakage" or loss of purity, but it is also the only way multiplicity can exist. The sensible world is the most distant point and therefore the "poorest" in being, but also what participates most fully in existence in its own form. Archaeoacoustics may offer quantitative insights into this concept. Measuring reverberation time (*RT60*) in different sacred spaces may provide a metric for sonic "retention" versus "leakage" [[679](https://www.academia.edu/68171229/Assessing_the_Acoustics_of_an_Ancient_Egyptian_Temple)]. A chamber with massive walls and little furnishing will have a long RT60, retaining sound; one with fabrics and furniture will have a short RT60, "dissipating" sound quickly. The choice of such a design could be intentional, designing an environment to promote specific mental states, whether prolonged contemplation (long RT60) or clarity and transience (short RT60).

The correct approach, as outlined in the project, is to use archaeoacoustics with rigorous protocols to measure natural modes, reverberation and relation to known ritual events, where possible. The correspondence with Vedic sacrifice and Pralaya cycles is far more robust, since it is firmly rooted in ancient textual corpora describing the function of these processes in maintaining the cosmos. The investigation should continue to build a negative list of invalid correlations to avoid contaminating the analysis with pseudoscience, such as associating random frequencies with DNA without empirical evidence.

## Uncertainty and Process: The Yi of the Yijing and the Pythagorean Leimma

The philosophical question for the $\eta$ term, "Does the ancient system admit a term that is neither law nor will?", questions the presence of randomness, contingency or fundamental "noise" in the structure of reality. This section explores how ancient traditions, particularly the Chinese and Greek, dealt with uncertainty and the residual, using analogies of the *Yijing* (I Ching), *yi* (process of change) and the *leimma* (Pythagorean remainder). These ideas serve as analogical correspondences for a term introducing an element of non-determinism or latent potentiality into the evolution of the system.

The **Yijing** (Book of Changes) is an exceptional source for this concept [[426](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/win2021/entries/chinese-change/)]. It is not a book of oracles in the sense of infallible predictions, but a system for navigating change and uncertainty [[506](https://chajournal.com/2022/02/17/yijing/)]. Its 64 hexagrams and 384 changing lines represent a spectrum of possibilities and dynamic trajectories [[503](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Yijing)]. The central concept of **yi** refers not only to "change", but to the underlying cosmic process guiding this continuous transformation [[631](https://sfl.seu.edu.cn/_upload/article/files/7b/58/35a5cd5e4901b78328743c67beca/e894a607-0f26-4df9-886a-11889980132a.pdf)]. The system operates by reading the "remainder" or residual of the universe — the small variations and circumstances that the greater law does not cover. Consulting the Yijing involves a practice of simplification and randomization (counting yarrow stalks or coins) to obtain a hexagram, an act introducing an element of calculated randomness into the decision process. The result is not a determination, but guidance, a reading of the latent potential in a situation. "Noise" here is not a defect to be eliminated, but the very raw material of change and opportunity. The correspondence with the $\eta$ term is therefore very strong: it represents the contribution of chance or the residual adding complexity and new possibilities to the deterministic dynamics of the other terms of the equation. The hexagram string can be seen as a representation of probabilities, analogous to the wave function of a quantum system [[340](https://philarchive.org/archive/LEOYAE)].

In Ancient Greece, the philosopher Pythagoras and his followers developed a musical theory based on integer proportions, such as 2:1 (octave), 3:2 (fifth) and 4:3 (fourth). However, they discovered that when attempting to build a complete scale (for example, an octave divided into twelve semitones), there was a small error or "remainder" that could not be explained by these harmonic proportions. This remainder, known as **leimma** (used in the context of 256:243, the difference between a ditone and two tones), represents what pure reason and mathematical law cannot close or resolve. The leimma is mathematical "noise", the residual excess which, paradoxically, is necessary for the richness and complexity of Western music. It introduces a calculated imperfection allowing modulation between keys. This Pythagorean "remainder" works as an analogy for the $\eta$ term, representing a contribution that does not follow the main law, but is indispensable for the complete structure. Oracles and lots in various cultures, including Greece and Egypt, also functioned as a reading of the residual, seeking signs in random or insignificant events to decipher the will of the gods or the future course.

The concept of **Anirvacanīya** in Advaita Vedanta offers a third perspective, although more metaphysical. Anirvacanīya, meaning "unspeakable" or "indefinable", is used to describe the nature of the phenomenal world and of ignorance (*avidyā*) [[261](https://www.advaita-vision.org/anirvacaniya/), [266](https://drsurabhisolanki.com/2026/05/17/what-is-anirvachaniya-in-advaita-vedanta-explained-simply/)]. The world cannot be adequately described as totally real or totally unreal; it transcends both categories [[262](https://www.reddit.com/r/AdvaitaVedanta/comments/1odtze0/the_world_is_anirvacan%C4%ABya/), [454](https://www.indiadivine.org/content/topic/1391800-definition-for-anirvachaniya/)]. Ignorance (*avidyā*), which is the root of suffering, is characterized as anirvacanīya because it cannot be reduced to nothing or to something; it is an "inexplicable fact of experience" for the mind [[268](http://www.advaita.org.uk/discourses/definitions/anirvachanIya.htm)]. Although not a physical "noise", Anirvacanīya represents a form of fundamental uncertainty about the very nature of reality, an epistemological gap that cannot be filled by dualist logic. The correspondence with the $\eta$ term is subtler, but may be interpreted as representing a superposition state or a region of ontological indefiniteness in the system.

The table below organizes the sources for uncertainty and process:

| Philosophical Concept | Civilization | Key Concept | Primary Source | Structural Analogy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Uncertainty/Process** | China | Yi (Process of Change) | Yijing (I Ching) | The system deals with change and uncertainty through a process of reading the residual. |
| **Uncertainty/Mathematical Noise** | Greece | Leimma (Pythagorean Remainder) | Euclid, Sectio Canonis | Reason cannot fully close the scale, leaving mathematical "noise". |

The investigation must be careful not to fall into the error of romanticizing the Greek view of reason versus hearing, as Aristoxenus did in contrast to the Pythagorean approach. Both approaches have their merits. The key is to understand that "noise" or "remainder" are not always negative. In the Yijing, they are the source of practical wisdom; in the musical system, they are the source of complex harmony. This perspective transforms the $\eta$ term from a mere noise problem into a functional resource, a source of potentiality and change. The correspondence with the Yijing is particularly powerful because it presents a complete operational system incorporating uncertainty as an active principle, not as a defect. This makes it an excellent structural analogy for the stochastic or noise term in a dynamic equation. The comparative analysis should explore how different cultures evaluated this residual element: was it a sign of divine imperfection, a source of human freedom or a fundamental creative principle?

However, the research also highlighted the importance of maintaining a negative list of invalid correlations to avoid esotericism. The association of arbitrary frequencies with DNA, for example, lacks foundation and must be discarded. The analysis must remain faithful to the texts and available empirical data.

In conclusion, the investigation demonstrated that ancient cosmologies were not merely mythological, but sophisticated systems of thought that developed analogical models for understanding the fundamental structure of reality. The quantum equation, although a product of the 20th century, works as a surprisingly effective conceptual map for navigating these structures. It allows a systematic comparison transcending linguistic and cultural barriers, revealing a remarkable convergence of ideas about origin, coupling, self-reference, memory, boundaries, dissipation and uncertainty. Future work should focus on executing the planned concrete projects — such as the Ratio Sheet and the detailed analysis of sun-cavity-axis sites — to generate empirical data that can further solidify these analogies and potentially lead to a more refined taxonomy of the ontological models of ancient civilizations.
