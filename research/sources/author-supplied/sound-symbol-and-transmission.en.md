# From Palaeolithic sound to runes: forty-five thousand years of frequency, geometry, symbol, memory and transmission in northern Europe

## Method, limits and evidence map

The most important conclusion of this investigation can be stated before entering the chronology: **the interval between the Aurignacian flutes and the first runes is not a "cultural void"**. It is a discontinuous record in which instruments, graphic marks, body ornaments, highly standardized technologies, burials, rock art, exceptionally preserved wood and fibres, watery depositions, astronomical objects and, finally, graphically encoded language survive at different times. What does **not** survive is a continuous chain capable of demonstrating that a specific meaning — a melody, a sign, a cosmology, a narrative — crossed forty millennia without rupture. citeturn1search2turn6search0turn22search4turn21search1

This requires distinguishing five levels in each case:

| Level | What it means in this investigation |
|---|---|
| **OBSERVABLE** | matter, dimensions, perforations, incisions, pigments, stratigraphic position, datings, residues, DNA, actually measured acoustic response |
| **INFERENCE** | function derived directly from form or wear: for example, "aerophone" |
| **INTERPRETATION** | plausible cultural function: performance, identity, ceremony, social marking |
| **HYPOTHESIS** | testable but undemonstrated explanation: calendar, shamanic ritual, specific cosmology |
| **MODERN RECONSTRUCTION** | replica, simulation, experimental tuning, staging, ethnographic analogy |

The confidence scale adopted is the one requested:

**[A]** directly documented or measured; **[B]** strongly supported; **[C]** probable; **[D]** possible; **[E]** controversial; **[F]** speculative/undemonstrated; **[G]** contradicted by the available evidence.

And, for continuity:

**demonstrated continuity** = archaeological intermediaries + transmission mechanism;  
**probable continuity** = relatively dense and compatible series;  
**possible continuity** = no contradiction, but intermediaries missing;  
**formal convergence** = same form, with no demonstrable genealogy;  
**no demonstrable relation** = there is no positive evidence of transmission.

### The first counterintuitive result

Deliberate geometry in the north of present-day Germany **predates the documented arrival of Homo sapiens in the region**. A giant-deer phalanx from Einhornhöhle received a systematic arrangement of incisions at least about 51 thousand years ago; experimental and microscopic analyses support that the marks were deliberately produced. Calling the pattern a "symbol" is a further interpretation, although supported by the authors. citeturn20search2turn20search10

There are even much older claims for Bilzingsleben, in central Germany, involving regular series of cuts in bone hundreds of thousands of years old. The deliberate character of part of the marks is defended in the literature, including institutionally by the Landesmuseum; their classification as "art", "symbol" or "abstract thought" is much more controversial. Therefore: **geometric marks [B/E depending on the specimen]; symbolic meaning [E]**. citeturn20search3turn20search14

Globally, Trinil, in Java, offers another important antecedent: a shell associated with *Homo erectus* bears deliberate geometric engraving much earlier than the Upper Palaeolithic. It demonstrates that the production of abstract form was not born with the runes, nor with Scandinavia, nor probably with *Homo sapiens*. It does not demonstrate any transmission to northern Europe. **Classification: global cognitive convergence/antecedent, not continuity.** citeturn4search0

## From the cold of forty-five thousand years ago to the end of the glaciation

Around 45 thousand years ago, Ranis, in present-day Thuringia, shows *Homo sapiens* episodically occupying a very cold landscape of steppe-tundra character, alongside reindeer, horse, woolly rhinoceros and bear. The Lincombian-Ranisian-Jerzmanowician sequences became associated with modern humans at dates of approximately 44–41 thousand calibrated years BP. Occupation seems to have been intermittent and spatially shared the landscape with large carnivores; this already prevents imagining a continuous "northern European civilization" beginning there. citeturn0search0turn0search3turn0search5

To the south, but still north of the Alps, the Swabian Jura preserves almost simultaneously an extraordinary association between **musical instruments, figurative art, ornaments and raw materials worked with enormous technical investment**. Geißenklösterle, Hohle Fels and Vogelherd thus constitute one of the first places where the question "sound + image + ornament + symbol?" can receive a positive material answer. What we do not know is whether these domains formed a unified religious system. citeturn1search2turn2search1

**Answer to the question "which is the oldest?":** if we require unequivocal, the answer is not Divje Babe. It is the **Aurignacian flute complex of the Swabian Jura**. Geißenklösterle has the potentially oldest contexts; Hohle Fels offers the most iconic nearly complete specimen. Saying "43,000 years exactly" for an individual flute would exceed dating resolution: we are dating layers and associations, not the instant the bone was perforated. citeturn1search1turn1search2

### What manufacture says about cognition, without needing to invoke religion

A bird long-bone flute requires selecting a suitable diaphysis, sectioning and smoothing the tube, preparing the blowing end and perforating holes in usable positions. An ivory flute is technologically more demanding because a curved solid must be worked into parts constituting a functional inner cavity. Recent reconstructions of the GK3 confirm that manufacture permits a playable instrument of the *notch flute* type. This demonstrates **operational chain, anticipation of the final form and empirical knowledge of the relation between tube, holes and sound**; it does not demonstrate formal mathematical theory. citeturn20search5turn20search1

Therefore:

> **knowing how to fabricate a proportional acoustic object [A/B] ≠ writing the physics of the object [G].**

Already in this period there is also portable figurative art, pendants and ornamented objects in the same archaeological complex. Thus the minimal proposition "these populations possessed sound + image + ornamentation + graphic repetition" is supported. The proposition "all were components of a single cosmological ritual" is not. citeturn1search2turn6search0

### Geometry: from individual marks to conventions

The Einhornhöhle find changes the starting point. The bone, with lines forming an arrangement of offset chevrons, was intentionally modified at least 51 thousand years ago, before the local evidence of *Homo sapiens*. The production sequence required several carefully oriented incisions. **Geometric form [A]; intention to decorate [B]; shared symbolism [C]; specific meaning [F].** citeturn20search2

In 2026, a particularly important quantitative study examined more than 3,000 marks on approximately 260 European Palaeolithic objects, many belonging to the early Upper Palaeolithic, approximately 43–34 ka. Crosses, strokes, dots, star/reticulated patterns and other elements do not appear simply as random noise: their distribution has enough structure for the authors to argue for **conventional signs**. At the same time, the statistical properties differ from those of modern writing, and no phonetic or lexical value has been demonstrated. citeturn6search0turn6search2turn6search13

This is a fundamental distinction:

**mark → repeated pattern → visual convention** is now a reasonably strong sequence;  
**visual convention → linguistic writing** is not.

### Counting and the "Palaeolithic calendar" problem

Notch series on Palaeolithic objects can certainly store **quantity**, sequence or episodes, but discovering that there are 13 marks does not prove that "13 = months", "13 = moons" or "13 = animals". The hypothesis published in 2023 according to which dots, strokes and Y-shaped signs associated with animals would encode a phenological calendar based on lunar months is ingenious, but received substantial methodological criticism, including concerning data selection and the relation between the model and the evidence. A later assessment also warned against accepting the interpretation as a demonstration of writing/calendar. citeturn7search0turn7search5turn7search7

Hence:

**observable numerical series [A] → possible quantitative storage [C/D] → lunar calendar [E] → writing [F/G].**

### Caves, fire, sound and art

Niaux, Le Portel and other caves were studied by Reznikoff and other researchers through vocalizations, echoes and resonances. There are cases in which visually marked regions coincide with strong acoustic responses and in which niches or corridors produce perceptible sound effects. These results make it plausible that Palaeolithic humans noticed and exploited subterranean acoustic properties. citeturn3search2turn3search0

But there is a serious methodological problem. A cave presents thousands of possible points; art, accessibility, paintable surfaces, circulation, lighting, chamber size and acoustics vary together. In the literature located in this investigation, the most famous results do not constitute a blind, sufficiently controlled multi-cave test permitting the claim that **acoustics caused the choice of panels**. Later archaeoacoustic studies have precisely emphasized the need for exhaustive methods, comparable measurements and control of sampling choices. citeturn3search13turn3search0

The correct classification is:

| Proposition | Result |
|---|---|
| Caves have dramatically different acoustic points | **[A]** |
| Palaeolithic humans could hear them | **[A/B]** |
| At some sites there is an art–resonance association | **[B/C]** |
| The general association exceeds chance across all Palaeolithic art | **[E]**, not demonstrated robustly enough |
| Caves were chosen primarily for acoustics | **[D/E]** |
| Echo = ritual | **[F]** without independent evidence |

Lascaux, Chauvet and Altamira must therefore be treated as sensorially complex spaces, not automatically as "acoustic temples".

## The northern Holocene: recolonization, the dead, animals and submerged landscapes

The largest chronological rupture in the narrative occurs here. The Aurignacian communities of the Swabian Jura cannot simply be transformed into "cultural ancestors of the Scandinavians". Then came the glacial maximum, radical environmental changes, population displacements and, in the far north, recolonization after the thaw. Scandinavia was one of the last large European regions to become habitable again after the Last Glacial Maximum. Genomic data indicate at least two large post-glacial movements: initially from the south and then from the northeast, producing the Scandinavian hunter-gatherers genetically mixed between western and eastern components. citeturn22search4turn22search5turn22search1

This is precisely where a supposed 40-thousand-year tradition loses its mechanism:

> **Palaeolithic occupation of central Europe ≠ continuous transmission to Holocene Scandinavia.**

Fosna/Hensbacka, Komsa and kindred traditions record knowledge of coastal mobility, navigation, hunting, fishing and lithic technology, but there is no evidence that they carried specific Aurignacian melodies or graphic codes. Similar technology can be transmitted between neighbouring populations or reinvented under similar problems; for a Palaeolithic melody we possess no intermediary. citeturn22search0turn22search4

Observe the character of the sequence: there is **much culture in each phase**, but there is no object that can be followed continuously from 40,000 BC to AD 100.

### Doggerland: what the sea removed from the sample

Doggerland is not a metaphor. Enormous parts of the continental shelf now covered by the North Sea were terrestrial and coastal landscape during the final Pleistocene and early Holocene. Sea-level rise progressively flooded this world. The Storegga tsunami, approximately 8,150–8,200 years ago, was regionally violent, but recent work rejects the simplistic idea that a single wave "destroyed all of Doggerland"; local topography and long-term marine transgression are essential. citeturn13search2turn22search10

The epistemologically important point is that **coastal settlements tend precisely to concentrate fishing activities, net manufacture, boats, wood, fibres and organic discard**. When the ancient coast goes to the seabed, the on-land archaeological map becomes a structurally biased sample. Submerged Baltic and Danish sites preserve wood, antler, organic remains and structures that would almost never survive in oxygenated soils. citeturn22search19turn17search32turn22search3

This became even more concrete in 2025, when underwater excavations in Aarhus Bay investigated a coastal settlement of about 8,500 years, preserving, among other materials, wood and animal remains in anoxic sediments. citeturn22news39

In 2024, the identification of the so-called Blinkerwall under the German Baltic — a line of stones nearly a kilometre long, probably built when that landscape was dry — showed that large-scale structures can remain invisible for millennia under the sea. Its most probable interpretation is a hunting-drive structure; not a ritual monument. citeturn22news40

Therefore, to the question "how much of the record is physically under the North Sea?", the rigorous answer is:

> **a culturally important, probably enormous area, but there is no archaeologically defensible percentage of lost culture.**

### Wood, leather, fibres and the error of confusing "not preserved" with "non-existent"

Differential preservation is one of the most important factors in the entire investigation.

| Support | Typical preservation | Northern European evidence | Potentially invisible information |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stone** | high | rock art, tools, plaques, runestones | engraved images and inscriptions survive disproportionately |
| **Bone/antler** | medium; poor in acid soils | instruments, points, frontlets, ornaments | instruments, marks, pendants |
| **Metal** | medium–high, but subject to corrosion and recycling | bronze, lurs, weapons, ornaments | images and inscriptions |
| **Wood** | low in oxygenated soil; excellent in anoxia | Tybrind Vig and submerged Baltic sites | boats, drums, plaques, marked staffs, sculptures |
| **Bark/bast** | very low | exceptional watery survivals | cordage, containers, nets, decorated surfaces |
| **Fibres/textile** | very low | Bronze Age textiles; Mesolithic microfibres | clothing, woven designs, identity patterns |
| **Leather/skin** | very low | indirect and microscopic traces in burials | drums, clothing, bags, painted surfaces |
| **Feathers/fur** | extreme | identified microscopically at Skateholm | headdresses, clothing, visual identity |

The submerged Danish sites directly demonstrate the magnitude of the bias: wood, bark and other organics appear when the physicochemical conditions are right. citeturn17search5turn17search32 The Skateholm analysis published in 2026 recovered microscopic hairs, feathers and fibres in burials where macroscopic objects of that kind had already disappeared. citeturn18search0turn18search9

Hence:

\[
\text{not found}\;\not\Rightarrow\;\text{did not exist}
\]

but also:

\[
\text{could have existed}\;\not\Rightarrow\;\text{existed}.
\]

This is especially important for **drums, strings, musical bows, wooden instruments, encoded textiles, knots and mnemonic staffs**. All are physically plausible; none should be retroactively placed in the northern Palaeolithic without a find.

### Death: when systematic treatment becomes visible

In the northern Holocene, the funerary evidence becomes extraordinarily detailed. Skateholm I and II, in southern Sweden, have dozens of human burials and separate dog burials, varied body positions and even cremations. The new microscopy published in 2026 showed that some of the dead were associated with skins and feathers, including materials in the head region compatible with adornments. citeturn18search0turn18search9

This demonstrates **formal treatment of the dead [A]**, use of animal materials in funerary presentation **[A/B]** and differentiation of burials **[A]**. "Belief in life after death" remains **[D]**, because grave goods do not preserve theological propositions.

A Mesolithic Skateholm grave with perforated wild-boar teeth was experimentally reconstructed as a rattling ornament. Movement of the teeth would produce sound, but the reconstruction does not prove how they were used in life nor that the sound possessed a specific ritual function. citeturn18search11

Thus appears one of the few concrete bridges between **body + animal + movement + potential sound + death**, but still very far from the claim that "bone, music and funerary cult constituted a system".

### Animals: when does fauna become more than food?

There is no single date.

At Ranis, reindeer, horse and other large mammals are mostly part of the detectable ecology and subsistence. citeturn0search0 In the Upper Palaeolithic, animals also appear as images and raw materials. In the Mesolithic, the picture becomes even more complex: at Star Carr, 21 modified red-deer skull/antler pieces — the famous *frontlets* — could be worn on the head. "Ritual mask" is only one of the interpretations; hunting disguise, performance or multiple uses remain possible. citeturn14search2

At Skateholm, dogs receive their own burials and feathers/skins enter some mortuary practices. citeturn18search0 At Alta, elk, reindeer, bears, fish, whales, birds and other animals are depicted alongside humans, boats, fishing and hunting. This demonstrates that animals already participated in visual systems far beyond simple zoological lists, but it does not give us their "mythological names". citeturn16search0

No secure chain permits:

**Palaeolithic bird → Odin's ravens**;  
**Mesolithic bear → Germanic bear cult**;  
**Bronze Age serpent → Jörmungandr**;  
**Star Carr deer → later Norse cosmology**.

They are, without specific intermediaries, of **no demonstrable relation**.

### The Sámi line must remain separate

Archaeogenetics indicates entry of Siberia-related ancestry into Fennoscandia at least about 3,500 years ago, later present in high proportion in populations that today speak Uralic languages. This is not equivalent to dating the Sámi language, Sámi cosmology or joik to 3,500 years ago; much less to the Palaeolithic. **DNA ≠ language ≠ religion ≠ identity.** citeturn19search0

Joik is an excellent ethnomusicological demonstration that an oral tradition can relate a melodic configuration to a person, place, animal or experience without depending on written notation. Cognitive studies show that its melodic organization differs in important respects from Western tonal expectations. This proves an anthropological possibility, not a Palaeolithic continuity. citeturn19search5turn19search33turn19search13

Likewise, the preserved Sámi drums are largely historical objects of the 17th–18th centuries; their documentation does not authorize projecting their designs, oracular function or cosmology forty thousand years back. citeturn19search2

## From the Neolithic to the Bronze Age: geometry, sky, boats, metal, water and sound

The entry of farming into southern Scandinavia, approximately in the fourth millennium BC, is not simply an economic change. Populations with strong Neolithic-farmer ancestry enter the region; later, another massive transformation introduces steppe-related ancestry. Recent genomic studies show that Denmark experienced very large demographic turnovers in relatively short space. citeturn12search3turn12search7turn12search10

This is directly relevant to memory transmission: a population turnover **may** break, transform or absorb traditions, but genomes do not tell us which song, symbol or narrative survived. A newly arrived population may adopt local practices; a genetically continuous population may change religion and language. Therefore no argument "DNA changed, hence the myth disappeared" is valid.

### Holocene geometry and rock art

Northern Europe provides an impressive visual series, but not an alphabet in linear evolution.

Alta records hunting, fishing, boat-travel scenes and an enormous variety of circumpolar fauna. The relation of the surfaces to ancient shorelines permits establishing relative chronologies in part of the tradition. citeturn16search0

Bardal, in central Norway, is especially instructive because iconographic traditions of different periods occupy the same rock support. A study of the shoreline chronology places some of the large naturalistic figures in the Late Mesolithic, around 5300 BC; later Bronze Age images may overlie earlier motifs. This is a case of **reuse of place**, not proof that meaning remained the same. citeturn16search27turn16search3

Nämforsen contains large quantities of elk, boats, fish, people and other elements; recent work discusses narrative changes and relations between hunting traditions and maritime images. citeturn16search5turn16search22

Tanum, approximately between 1700 and 300 BC in its main tradition, offers boats, people, weapons, animals, footprints, circles and *cup-marks*. Motif repetition indicates shared visual conventions. The step from "convention" to "writing" remains unfounded, because the motifs possess no demonstrable linguistic values. citeturn16search29turn16search19

We can thus write:

\[
\text{repeated figure}
\rightarrow
\text{iconographic convention}
\rightarrow
\text{social information}
\]

with reasonable confidence; but not:

\[
\text{boat}
\rightarrow
\text{encoded word "boat"}.
\]

### Vasagård and the radiated symbols

Vasagård, on Bornholm, became particularly important after the 2025 publication on more than 600 whole or fragmentary engraved plaques, deposited in a Neolithic enclosure in the early third millennium BC. Many specimens have radiated patterns, hence the modern term "sun stones". citeturn15search18

Mass deposition is observable. Radiated form is observable. A chronological association with a major volcanic disturbance and reduced sunlight was proposed by an interdisciplinary team. The interpretation that the plaques were "sacrificed to bring the Sun back" is already cultural hypothesis, not measurement. citeturn15search10

Classification:

**radiated plaques [A] → possible solar representation [B/C] → crisis-related solar ritual [C/D] → specific solar deity [F].**

### Nebra: when the sky becomes almost impossible to deny

The Nebra Sky Disc is a much stronger case of astronomical representation. The object is bronze with gold applications; its deposition occurred approximately in 1600 BC, at the end of the central European Early Bronze Age. A 2024 archaeometallurgical investigation reinforces the material picture and the technological history of the disc. citeturn15search9

At a minimum, we observe:

**[A]** metal disc;  
**[A]** golden applications forming a crescent, a large circular element, numerous small discs and arcs added in phases;  
**[B]** ensemble representing celestial phenomena;  
**[C]** group of seven dots as the Pleiades;  
**[B/C]** lateral arcs related to the solar horizon;  
**[C]** practical calendrical use;  
**[D]** specific theology or priesthood.

Dating underwent an important controversy in 2020, when an Iron Age attribution was proposed. Interdisciplinary responses defended the Bronze Age context again, and later work continues to treat deposition as approximately 1600 BC. citeturn15search11turn15search9

Nebra is therefore very different from a bone with 29 scratches: **the graphic ensemble is explicitly celestial with a much higher degree of security**, although details of its operation remain debatable.

### Trundholm: Sun, horse and movement

The Trundholm Sun Chariot was produced approximately in 1400 BC. The object combines a bronze horse, a disc gilded on one side and wheels. Spirals and circular patterns cover part of the surface. The interpretation as a representation of the Sun transported by a horse is strongly supported by the ensemble, but "which myth exactly?" remains unrecoverable. citeturn15search1

Here it becomes legitimate to speak of a **Nordic Bronze Age solar cosmology in a broad sense [B]**, because the representation is not an isolated circle: there are disc, gold, horse, movement and regional iconographic parallels. Even so:

> Trundholm **is not** evidence of Sól, Skinfaxi, Árvakr or any named character of the much later sources.

The "disc + horse" motif may have a long history in the region; the identity of a specific narrative does not survive.

### Boats: technology and symbol simultaneously

Nordic rock art turns boats into one of the most repeated motifs of the Bronze Age. This does not require choosing between "realistic" or "religious". Coastal peoples really navigated, traded and transported people; precisely for this reason the vessel could acquire metaphorical or cosmological value. Tanum, Nämforsen and Alta show different traditions of naval representation. citeturn16search0turn16search5turn16search29

The correct rule is:

**real boat [A] + depicted boat [A] → cultural importance [B] → cosmological voyage of the dead [C/D depending on context] → specific Norse myth [F].**

### Textiles: the great lost visual archive

Nordic Bronze Age textiles survive in exceptional quantity in some funerary contexts. Isotopic analyses indicated that at least three quarters of the wool samples examined in a Danish study had values compatible with origin outside the local area analysed, showing circulation networks much more complex than an exclusively domestic economy. citeturn17search2

Weaving requires:

\[
\text{sequence}
+
\text{counting}
+
\text{tension}
+
\text{repetition}
+
\text{symmetry}.
\]

This is **operational mathematics** in a cognitive sense: the producer must maintain discrete relations and repetitions. It is not evidence of written numbers or theorems.

The archaeological implication is large: textiles may have carried patterns of identity, status and aesthetics almost completely absent from the sample. The new microscopic evidence from Skateholm demonstrates that even skins and feathers could structure bodily appearance seven thousand years ago while leaving very little macroscopic trace. citeturn18search0

There is no foundation, however, for postulating a pre-runic Scandinavian "textile code" without repeated examples correlatable to meanings.

### Water, bogs and deposition without recovery

Water becomes particularly important in the northern record for two different reasons, which must not be confused.

First, **preservation**: peat, anoxic sediments and submerged bottoms conserve organic material. citeturn17search27turn17search32

Second, **deliberate human deposition**: in the southern Mesolithic there are already assemblages analysed as extraordinary deposits or "hoards", while in the Bronze and Iron Ages the deposition of weapons, instruments and other goods in wetlands is much more conspicuous. A study of 124 southern Scandinavian Mesolithic deposits discusses precisely how far such acts were ritualized. citeturn17search11

The Danish lurs are the most extraordinary sonorous example: the National Museum records 39 Danish specimens, normally found in **pairs**, and the Danish finds are associated with bogs. citeturn9search18

The pattern:

\[
\text{water}
+
\text{valuable object}
+
\text{deposition}
+
\text{non-recovery}
\]

is, for certain periods and artefact classes, strongly supported. The meaning — offering to a deity, closure of use, collective memory, display of wealth — cannot be determined from the water alone.

### Lurs and the peak of metallic sound

Lurs are cast-bronze tubes, with bells and geometry suited to excitation by lip vibration. Their metallurgical complexity, large dimensions and frequency of pair finds make them the best case of material **sonic standardization** in pre-runic Scandinavia. citeturn9search18

Modern acoustic experiments demonstrate that lurs produce multiple resonances and notes related to the tube's resonance series, although real geometry, bell and the musician's technique deviate the instrument from an ideal cylindrical model. Studies of specimens such as Brudevælte measured pitch, timbre and loudness. citeturn9search9

"Why pairs?"

**Pair finds [A].**  
**Probably played in coordinated fashion [C].**  
**Ceremonial deposition [B/C].**  
**Military use [D].**  
**Representation of cosmological duality [F] without further evidence.**

## Frequency, harmonics, resonance and archaeoacoustics

The "frequency" part requires conceptual cleanup. A prehistoric instrument does not keep a frequency the way a modern tuning fork does. Frequency depends on internal geometry, hole positions, end conditions, temperature, blowing pressure and technique.

For an ideal cylindrical tube open at both ends:

\[
f_n \approx \frac{nc}{2L_{\mathrm{eff}}}
\]

and:

\[
\lambda_n=\frac{c}{f_n}.
\]

For an ideal tube approximately closed at one end:

\[
f_n \approx \frac{(2n-1)c}{4L_{\mathrm{eff}}}.
\]

Here, \(L_{\mathrm{eff}}\) is not simply the length measured with a ruler. It includes end corrections, and in flutes with finger holes the acoustic distribution is even more complex.

The width of a resonance can be described by the factor:

\[
Q=\frac{f_0}{\Delta f},
\]

and the input acoustic impedance, conceptually,

\[
Z(\omega)=\frac{p(\omega)}{U(\omega)},
\]

relates acoustic pressure and volumetric flow velocity. Impedance/response peaks identify modes that the excitation mechanism can sustain.

These equations are **modern physical models**. A Palaeolithic artisan did not need to know them to learn empirically that:

\[
L\downarrow \;\Rightarrow\; f\uparrow
\]

or that opening a hole closer to the blowing point effectively shortens the vibrating column.

### The oldest frequency: what can and cannot be said

A study/reconstruction of the Geißenklösterle flute reported, in replicas and experimental conditions, pitches approximately in the range of **1,760 to 2,790 Hz**, depending on hole, embouchure and pressure. The publication itself stresses the variability of sound production. Later experimental work also emphasizes that different reconstructions can produce different results. citeturn20search9turn20search5

Using \(c\approx343\,m/s\) only as an illustrative physical calculation:

\[
\lambda(1760)\approx0.195\,m
\]

and

\[
\lambda(2790)\approx0.123\,m.
\]

But the correct sentence is:

> **these are frequencies of modern reconstructions geometrically associated with the artefact; they are not recordings or measurements of sound emitted by the original 40 thousand years ago.**

There is therefore no preserved "42,000-year-old note".

This also resolves the question of 432 Hz, 528 Hz and the like: **I found no archaeological or acoustic evidence supporting any recurring Palaeolithic tuning at those values.** The search for such numbers would be anachronistic. Published reconstructions vary with musician and instrument model; no absolute reference system comparable to "A=440 Hz" can be demonstrated. citeturn20search5turn20search0

### Is there a Palaeolithic scale?

There are instruments with multiple deliberately placed holes, so there is **capacity to produce differentiated pitch sets [A]**. Reconstructions can produce intervals that modern listeners recognize as musical. This does not prove "pentatonic scale", "diatonic", "temperament" or a shared absolute tuning.

To demonstrate Palaeolithic musical standardization one would need several complete, contemporary instruments from comparable contexts, with well-preserved dimensions, whose acoustic properties converged beyond what ergonomics and raw material would predict. The corpus does not permit this with high confidence.

Thus:

**multiple deliberate pitches [B]**;  
**repeatable intervals in replicas [B]**;  
**shared Palaeolithic scale system [D/E]**;  
**universal frequency pattern [F/G].**

### Sound + geometry: is there a real link?

Yes, but the demonstrable link is physical, not numerological.

In instruments:

\[
\text{length}
\rightarrow
L_{\mathrm{eff}}
\rightarrow
\text{resonances}
\rightarrow
\text{pitches}.
\]

In caves:

\[
\text{volume + shape + openings + absorption}
\rightarrow
\text{modes + echoes + reverberation}.
\]

In metal instruments:

\[
\text{tube profile + length + bell}
\rightarrow
\text{impedance}
\rightarrow
\text{set of playable modes}.
\]

A population without writing can master these relations through copying and error correction. The very success of a manufacture chain is information stored in practice.

### Archaeoacoustics: the most rigorous result possible

The classic studies at Niaux, Le Portel and other caves found suggestive relations between resonance points and concentrations of signs/images. citeturn3search2 More recent archaeoacoustic work expanded methodology through instrumental measurements, but the Palaeolithic literature still presents great heterogeneity of protocols. citeturn3search13

The test requested by the user — "beyond chance?" — must answer:

**Not demonstrated in a general and robust way for all studied Palaeolithic caves.**

What can be sustained is that some local correlations are real enough to justify investigation; not that "Palaeolithic people chose caves for their frequencies".

There is also no "ritual frequency" transversal to the sites. Mode frequencies depend on local dimensions; two different chambers naturally have different spectra.

### Sounding stone is not automatically a lithophone

The same rule applies to lithophones: many rocks present resonant modes when struck. To classify them archaeologically as instruments, repeated percussion marks in sonorous zones, context and, ideally, a use pattern incompatible with utilitarian activities are desirable.

Therefore:

\[
\text{stone resonates} \neq \text{stone was an instrument}.
\]

### The drum problem

A typical drum combines skin and wood — exactly two of the material classes with the worst preservation. Consequently, the absence of complete Palaeolithic or Mesolithic drums in the North is **not** strong evidence of absent percussion.

But the Sámi analogy does not resolve the gap either. The historically preserved Sámi drums are thousands or tens of thousands of years later than the Palaeolithic contexts. citeturn19search2

The classification is:

**body percussion/object striking as human capacity [A, trivially possible]; northern European Palaeolithic drums [D]; continuity to the Sámi drum [F].**

## Information before writing: marks, memory, orality and transmission

The most productive question is not "was there writing?" but:

> **what kinds of information could be stored and retrieved without writing?**

The archaeological answer is: a lot.

### An observable hierarchy

The minimal supported sequence is:

\[
\text{deliberate mark}
\rightarrow
\text{repeated mark}
\rightarrow
\text{pattern}
\rightarrow
\text{visual convention}
\rightarrow
\text{shared representation}
\rightarrow
\text{possible notation}
\rightarrow
\text{linguistic writing}.
\]

The first four or five levels exist long before runes. The final leap only becomes demonstrable when graphemes can be systematically associated with linguistic values.

The 2026 work on Palaeolithic signs is particularly important because it suggests that marks on Aurignacian objects were more structured and conventional than a view of "occasional doodles" would permit. At the same time, the patterns themselves do not behave like a modern writing system and have not been phonetically deciphered. citeturn6search0turn6search2

### Was there protowriting?

It depends on the definition.

With a very broad definition — "conventional graphic system that stores information without fully encoding speech" — some Palaeolithic, Neolithic or Bronze Age ensembles **may** be described as graphic protosystems.

With the strict criterion requested:

\[
\text{grapheme}
+
\text{reproducible linguistic value}
+
\text{sequence}
+
\text{shared convention},
\]

there is no demonstrated northern European protowriting before the first alphabetic/runic inscriptions.

The safer term is **sign system**, **iconography**, **possible notation** or **conventional mark**, as the case may be.

### Counting

Archaeology can recognize deliberate notch series; recognizing what was counted is much harder. The proposed Palaeolithic calendar case shows exactly the danger of turning numerical regularity into semantics without intermediaries. citeturn7search0turn7search5turn7search7

Thus:

> Is there evidence of capacity for quantitative repetition and grouping? **Yes.**  
> Is there a deciphered northern European numerical system before runes? **No.**  
> Is there a demonstrated Palaeolithic calendar? **No.**

### Marked wood

Watery sites prove that wooden objects were far more abundant than the terrestrial record suggests. Tybrind Vig and other sites preserve vessels, paddles and multiple organic objects; German Baltic sites produced large wood and antler assemblages. citeturn17search5turn22search7

But this investigation found no northern pre-runic corpus of **wooden staffs with a decipherable and repeated notch code** comparable to a formal notation.

This is an important answer because it avoids the two opposite errors:

"wood rotted, hence there must have been writing on wood" — **[F]**;

"we found no writing on wood, hence nobody marked wood" — also unjustified.

### Cord, net, knot and implicit mathematics

Producing net or textile requires repetition cycles, local counts, controlled tension and patterns. Cordage and nets are directly documented at prehistoric sites when preservation permits. citeturn17search3turn17search32

This is a form of **information embodied in technique**. A net transmits its construction algorithm physically: an apprentice can copy it without any notation. The same holds for boat, casting, weaving or flute.

It is a decisive distinction:

> **cultural information ≠ text.**

### Technology as memory

A craft tradition contains at least:

\[
\text{raw-material selection}
\rightarrow
\text{operation order}
\rightarrow
\text{gestures}
\rightarrow
\text{proportions}
\rightarrow
\text{quality control}.
\]

If a community can reproduce thousands of points, vessels or textiles with recurrent rules, there is a social memory of technique even if no individual can describe it mathematically.

Transmission can occur by demonstration, imitation and correction. This kind of memory is particularly resistant because the finished artefact functions as a "model", but it can break rapidly when specialists, social networks or raw materials disappear.

### Sound as memory

Ethnography, cognitive psychology and orality studies show that rhythm, repetition, formulaicity, melody and dance can serve as mnemonic technologies. A synthesis on primary orality identifies precisely verbal techniques, instrumental rhythmic accompaniment and dance among the mechanisms sustaining oral knowledge. citeturn19search7

Joik demonstrates, in the historical/ethnographic Sámi context, that a melodic form can operate as a representation linked to a person or entity without depending on writing. citeturn19search33turn19search13

But inference must stop there:

**music can store/evoke information [A/B experimentally/ethnographically]**;  
**prehistoric peoples probably used orality [B]**;  
**a given song survived since the Palaeolithic [F].**

### How long does orality preserve information?

There is no universal limit of "X generations". Fidelity depends on institutionalization, repetition, collective correction, relevance, genre structure and linguistic change. Narratives can preserve elements for a long time, but can simultaneously incorporate updates and reorganizations.

Archaeologically, this means that **a medieval narrative cannot be used alone to fill a gap of five, ten or forty thousand years**.

### A useful but not directly measurable loss model

The model proposed by the user is conceptually valid:

\[
I_{t+1}=I_t-L_t+R_t
\]

where \(I_t\) is the cultural repertoire, \(L_t\) the loss and \(R_t\) reproduction/renewal.

The problem is that archaeology does not observe the whole \(I_t\); it observes only a fraction:

\[
A_t=P_t(I_t)
\]

where \(P_t\) represents archaeological preservation, discovery and recognition.

An apparent fall in \(A_t\) may result from:

\[
L_t\uparrow
\]

or simply from:

\[
P_t\downarrow.
\]

This is exactly what Doggerland, Tybrind Vig and Skateholm demonstrate. When preservation changes, entire categories of culture reappear. citeturn22news39turn17search5turn18search0

### Where are ruptures particularly likely?

We cannot directly observe \(L_t>R_t\), but there are periods in which continuity of specific traditions must be treated with maximum caution: glacial changes and post-glacial recolonization; marine transgression and submersion of ancient coasts; entry of farmers; large later population turnovers; incorporation of steppe ancestry; and entry, in the far north, of new eastern ancestries. citeturn22search4turn12search7turn19search0

Again: these transformations make continuity **problematic**, not impossible.

## From images to writing: how runes really enter the record

The transition to runes is a qualitative leap because, for the first time, geometric forms can be systematically associated with units of a Germanic language.

A Palaeolithic straight line, cross or zigzag is not ancestral to a rune merely because a rune is also angular. Straight lines are particularly economical when engraving wood, bone, metal or stone. Without phonetic intermediaries, formal similarity is only **formal convergence**.

### The literate environment preceding runes

In the centuries around the turn of the era, northern Germanic communities maintained intense contact with the Roman world and, directly or indirectly, with Mediterranean graphic traditions. Runological studies consider that the alphabetic principle was adopted from a Mediterranean alphabet — Latin and north Italic traditions being the central candidates, although the exact model remains discussed. citeturn11search0turn21search14

This provides the mechanism missing from "rock art origin" theories:

\[
\text{contact with alphabet users}
\rightarrow
\text{knowledge that signs encode sounds}
\rightarrow
\text{selection/adaptation of forms}
\rightarrow
\text{Germanic alphabet}.
\]

This mechanism is historically and graphically plausible. We do not know the inventor's identity, the exact place, or whether there was a single act of creation.

### Negau and Meldorf

Pre-runic and borderline inscriptions matter precisely because they show contact between language, writing and the Germanic world.

The Meldorf fibula, from the first half of the 1st century AD, is a celebrated candidate for a very early runic inscription, but its reading is disputed: the signs can be understood as runes, protorunic forms or Latin letters. Therefore it cannot be used as "the first certain rune". **[E].** citeturn21search22turn21search6

This case illustrates something important: the transition probably did not look, in the real record, like flicking a switch between "no writing" and "writing". In contact environments, graphic experimentation is precisely what we would expect.

### Svingerud/Hole changes the chronology of use in stone

The 2025 publication on the Hole/Svingerud fragments, in Norway, is now central. Inscribed sandstone fragments were recovered in association with a burial ground whose radiocarbon contexts cover approximately **50 BC–AD 275**, providing the oldest currently known context for a runestone. citeturn21search1

It is vital not to turn:

\[
\text{context date}
\]

into

\[
\text{exact incision date}.
\]

Stratigraphic and radiocarbon modelling places relevant phases of the stone in the first centuries around the turn of the era, but the various inscriptions may have been made at different moments and even by different people. Some strokes are unequivocally harder to interpret than others. citeturn10search0turn10search6

This is precisely the prudence requested by the user: **the stone is datable; the instant of each scratch is not.**

### Vimose and the Funen knife

Vimose, on Funen, produced a set of Elder Futhark inscriptions of the 2nd–3rd centuries AD, including the famous comb with *harja*. In chronological traditions before Svingerud, it was frequently cited as the oldest runically datable specimen. citeturn21search4

In 2024, an iron knife found in a funerary context near Odense, approximately of the 2nd century AD, was announced, with five runes read as *hirila*. It reinforces Funen as one of the earliest foci of runic literacy. citeturn21news34

Therefore "first rune" must now be answered by category:

**oldest known runic context in stone:** Svingerud/Hole;  
**objects with unequivocally linguistic early inscriptions:** corpus of the early Roman Iron Age, including Funen/Vimose and new finds;  
**possibly earlier candidate:** Meldorf, but ambiguous.

### Rune as information technology

The decisive transformation is not that lines "become more complex". In fact, many runes are geometrically simple.

The transformation is:

\[
\text{form}
\rightarrow
\text{conventional phonological value}
\rightarrow
\text{sequence}
\rightarrow
\text{word}
\rightarrow
\text{linguistic retrieval}.
\]

This is the threshold that no spiral, *cup-mark*, Tanum boat or Palaeolithic zigzag has demonstrated crossing.

Runes are therefore first an **encoding technology for language**. Magical, votive or protective use may exist in specific inscriptions, but must not be used as the definition of runic writing.

### Is there a link between pre-runic symbols and runes?

Not a demonstrable link of graphic descent.

There is, naturally, human continuity in the very generic practice of:

**scratching → repeating → recognizing forms → marking objects.**

There may also have been continuity of social habits such as marking property or identity. But **phonetic literacy** finds a much stronger mechanism in contacts with Mediterranean alphabets than in Nordic Bronze symbols. citeturn11search0turn21search14

The classification is:

| Proposal | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Some runes resemble prehistoric forms | **[A]** |
| Resemblance proves descent | **[G]** |
| There is a general tradition of marking objects before runes | **[A/B]** |
| That tradition may have eased acceptance of a writing system | **[D]** |
| The phonetic principle came from Palaeolithic conventions | **[F/G]** |
| The alphabetic principle was adapted under contact with existing alphabets | **[B]** citeturn11search0turn21search14 |

### And the later deities?

The main investigation must stop here, as requested. It is crucial not to use Odin, Yggdrasil or the Edda to retroactively decipher the Bronze Age or the Mesolithic.

The fact that later Germanic texts and inscriptions name gods does not make an earlier horse, bird, tree, spear or disc a representation of one of those deities. Between **image without caption** and **linguistically named being** there is an enormous epistemological difference.

Thus, even when later iconographies combine man, horse and bird, the sequence must not be inverted:

\[
\text{similar old image}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{old Odin}.
\]

The emergence of documented theonyms belongs to a historical and epigraphic corpus later than this report's central threshold; it confirms that Germanic societies eventually textualized names that previously, if they existed, were transmitted by non-written means. It does not permit dating those names to the Palaeolithic.

## Continuity, rupture, recent discoveries and final answers

### What really remained?

The strongest continuities are not mythological; they are **structural and technological**.

The first is transmitted manufacture. From Palaeolithic tools to boats, nets, weaving, metallurgy and instruments, we observe techniques requiring learned and reproduced sequences.

The second is the use of the body as a support of social information: Palaeolithic pendants, Mesolithic teeth and feathers, later clothing and ornaments. The discovery of microscopic fibres at Skateholm reinforces that prehistoric bodily appearance was much richer than skeletons suggested. citeturn18search0turn18search9

The third is the deliberate production of sound. Instruments change radically in material and form, but the fabrication of acoustic devices is demonstrable at different times. This is continuity of **sonic technological capacity**, not of musical repertoire. citeturn1search2turn9search18

The fourth is the habit of externalizing information in objects and surfaces: incisions, patterns, rock art, iconography, finally writing. But the concrete systems present enough ruptures to prevent treating this sequence as a single graphic tradition. citeturn6search0turn16search0turn21search1

The fifth is repeated investment in places: caves, coasts, rapids, cemeteries, bogs and engraving fields. This permanence of **landscape as memory** is archaeologically visible; meanings may change while the place remains important.

### What only looks like continuity?

| Parallel | Classification |
|---|---|
| Palaeolithic zigzag → angular rune | **formal convergence / no demonstrable relation** |
| Neolithic circle → Sun in all cases | **not demonstrable without context** |
| radiated motif → Germanic solar deity | **possible continuity at most; theological identity undemonstrated** |
| Trundholm horse → horse of a specific Norse god | **no demonstrable relation** |
| Palaeolithic birds → Odin's ravens | **no demonstrable relation** |
| Mesolithic deer/antler → Germanic myth | **no demonstrable relation** |
| prehistoric tree → Yggdrasil | **no demonstrable relation** |
| ancient-art serpent → Jörmungandr | **no demonstrable relation** |
| Palaeolithic geometry → Futhark | **contradicted as direct genealogy by the absence of a chain and by the external alphabetic model** |
| Aurignacian flute → "Nordic" music | **no demonstrable musical continuity** |
| cave acoustics → shamanic rite | **possible acoustic use; specific rite speculative** |
| joik → Palaeolithic survival | **undemonstrated** |

### What disappeared?

Physical loss is brutally selective. Stone tends to remain; sound does not. Metal remains better than skin, but can be recycled. Wood can disappear completely in aerated soil and survive almost intact in anoxia. The record therefore over-represents **stone, bone and metal** and under-represents systems based on **voice, gesture, wood, leather, fibres, body paint, textiles and submerged coastal landscapes**. citeturn17search5turn18search0turn22news39

It is not possible to estimate honestly "60%", "90%" or any other percentage of lost information. We do not know the denominator.

Doggerland adds loss by submersion. The thaw adds territorial reconfiguration. Populations and social networks change. Technologies cease to be taught. Languages disappear. Symbols may continue as forms and lose meaning — or acquire new meaning.

Thus the "gap" has at least five components:

\[
\text{decomposition}
+
\text{submersion}
+
\text{sampling}
+
\text{social rupture}
+
\text{semantic change}.
\]

### Recent discoveries that change the picture

**2026 — Palaeolithic signs:** the analysis of more than 3,000 marks on approximately 260 objects reinforced the idea that some of the 43–34 ka graphic marks were structured and conventional, although they do not constitute demonstrated writing. It is one of the discoveries most directly relevant to the question "what was there between the flute and writing?". citeturn6search0turn6search13

**2026 — Skateholm:** microscopic techniques recovered hairs, feathers and fibres from Mesolithic burials, demonstrating experimentally that entire categories of material culture can "disappear" macroscopically and still leave microtraces. citeturn18search0turn18search9

**2026 — Doggerland:** recent sedimentary-DNA analyses of North Sea cores are refining the ecological reconstruction of the submerged landscape and suggesting more complex environments than uniformly tundra models. This does not prove human settlements at the sampled points, but increases the resolution of the world lost under the North Sea. citeturn22news38

**2025 — submerged Aarhus:** new excavations of a settlement of about 8,500 years expanded the evidence of exceptional coastal organic preservation under Danish waters. citeturn22news39

**2025 — Svingerud/Hole:** the reassembly and contextual publication of the fragments showed that the tradition of engraving runes in stone may reach the centuries around the turn of the era, well before the great Viking runestones. citeturn21search1

**2025 — Vasagård:** the integrated publication of the hundreds of radiated Bornholm plaques related their concentrated deposition to a period of strong climatic disturbance, offering a testable hypothesis of ritual response to a celestial/climatic event. Cultural causality remains inferential. citeturn15search18turn15search10

### The final evidence map

**Strongly documented:** modern humans in cold environments ~45 ka; Neanderthal deliberate geometry ~51 ka; Aurignacian flutes; art and ornamentation; post-glacial networks; organic technologies when preserved; complex Mesolithic burials; northern rock art; textiles; metallurgy; lurs; Bronze celestial representations; contact with alphabets; first runes. citeturn0search5turn20search2turn1search2turn18search0turn9search18turn15search9turn21search1

**Probable:** prehistoric societies used music, gesture, repetition and teaching as memory; certain images formed shared conventions; some watery depositions had non-utilitarian functions; populations perceived acoustic properties of the spaces they frequented.

**Possible:** some mark series stored specific quantities; some caves were intentionally exploited for acoustics; entirely lost organic objects included instruments, pictorial or mnemonic surfaces.

**Speculative:** specific Palaeolithic calendars; lost written systems on wood; continuity of melodies over tens of millennia; correspondence between Palaeolithic symbols and Germanic mythology.

**Undemonstrated/contradicted:** 432 Hz or 528 Hz as prehistoric tuning; zigzags as rune ancestors; "universal Palaeolithic pentatonism"; rock art as direct illustration of the Edda; joik or the Sámi drum as intact Palaeolithic fossils; all painted caves selected for resonance.

### The final first-principles test

Now let us temporarily remove all period, people, language and god names.

What remains physically?

A human takes matter and modifies it.

First, we observe:

\[
\text{human}
\rightarrow
\text{repeats gesture}
\rightarrow
\text{produces form}.
\]

Then:

\[
\text{form}
\rightarrow
\text{is copied}
\rightarrow
\text{becomes technique}.
\]

In some objects:

\[
\text{length}
+
\text{cavity}
+
\text{hole}
\rightarrow
\text{reproducible sound}.
\]

No written equation is needed: trial, listening and copying suffice.

In others:

\[
\text{scratch}
\rightarrow
\text{series}
\rightarrow
\text{pattern}
\rightarrow
\text{convention}.
\]

In others:

\[
\text{body}
+
\text{ornament}
\rightarrow
\text{visible social information}.
\]

When someone dies:

\[
\text{body}
\rightarrow
\text{deliberate position}
+
\text{object}
+
\text{pigment}
+
\text{clothing}
\rightarrow
\text{materialized social memory}.
\]

When knowledge must survive:

\[
\text{person}
\rightarrow
\text{demonstration}
+
\text{repetition}
+
\text{rhythm}
+
\text{object}
+
\text{place}
\rightarrow
\text{new person}.
\]

With larger technical networks:

\[
\text{copy}
+
\text{correction}
+
\text{standardization}
\rightarrow
\text{tradition}.
\]

With sky observation:

\[
\text{perceived cycle}
\rightarrow
\text{image}
\rightarrow
\text{possible material model}.
\]

Finally a qualitatively different innovation appears:

\[
\text{visual form}
\rightarrow
\text{conventional linguistic sound}
\rightarrow
\text{phonological sequence}
\rightarrow
\text{word recoverable by a stranger}.
\]

**This last leap is alphabetic writing.**

Now we can restore the historical categories.

Deliberate geometry already appears among Neanderthals; Aurignacian modern humans combine music, sculpture, ornament and graphic systems; the northern Holocene adds coastal networks, wood, navigation, cemeteries, rock art and new technologies; the Neolithic multiplies monuments, pottery and iconography; the Bronze Age integrates metallurgy, monumental sound, continental networks and exceptionally explicit celestial representations; the Iron Age, in intense contact with literate societies, introduces a Germanic code in which a form ceases to be only figure and begins to systematically represent language. citeturn1search2turn22search4turn15search18turn15search9turn9search18turn21search1

This is the **minimal sequence of really observable informational-complexity increase**:

\[
\boxed{
\text{gesture}
\rightarrow
\text{technique}
\rightarrow
\text{mark}
\rightarrow
\text{pattern}
\rightarrow
\text{convention}
\rightarrow
\text{representation}
\rightarrow
\text{possible notation}
\rightarrow
\text{phonetic grapheme}
\rightarrow
\text{text}
}
\]

It is not a single cultural lineage. Each arrow may contain extinction, reinvention, contact, population replacement and meaning change.

The final answer to the first great question is therefore:

> **Between the bone and ivory flutes of approximately 43–40 thousand years and the first runes, we can reconstruct societies capable of empirical acoustic engineering, ornamentation, figurative and abstract representation, non-textual mnemonic technologies, formal treatment of the dead, navigation and sophisticated organic fabrication, conventional rock art, weaving and operational counting, deliberate deposition of objects, sky observation, metallurgy, bronze instruments and continental transmission networks. What we cannot reconstruct is a continuous semantic chain linking a Palaeolithic melody, sign or cosmology to the Germanic traditions of the first centuries AD.** citeturn1search2turn6search0turn18search0turn16search0turn15search9turn9search18turn21search1

And the answer to the second question is equally clear:

> **The nearly forty-thousand-year "void" does not represent absent cultural complexity. It represents a combination of low chronological resolution, extremely selective preservation, submersion of ancient landscapes, destruction of organic materials, uneven archaeological sampling, environmental changes, population turnovers and transmission ruptures. At the same time, poor preservation does not authorize inventing continuities: what disappeared may have been culturally very rich, but its specific contents remain unknown.** The recurrent discovery of wood at submerged sites and, in 2026, of microscopic feathers and fibres in burials that seemed to have lost them is a particularly strong material demonstration of this bias. citeturn17search5turn22news39turn18search0

The most rigorous result is not "everything was connected" nor "nothing was connected". It is this:

\[
\boxed{
\text{OBJECT}
\rightarrow
\text{FORM}
\rightarrow
\text{FUNCTION}
\rightarrow
\text{PATTERN}
\rightarrow
\text{REPETITION}
\rightarrow
\text{TRANSMISSION}
}
\]

and, only then, asking:

\[
\boxed{
\text{CONTINUITY?}
\quad\text{or}\quad
\text{RUPTURE?}
}
\]

For **flute → rune**, the answer is almost total semantic rupture between distinct systems. For **the human capacity to turn proportion into sound, repetition into technique, form into convention and convention into memory**, continuity is much deeper — not because the same myth survived forty thousand years, but because the record repeatedly shows human beings externalizing knowledge in **bodies, objects, materials, landscapes, sounds and, finally, written language**.
